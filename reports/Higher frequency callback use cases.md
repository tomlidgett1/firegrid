# Sell the Callback to Venue Groups First

Go down the business path, but not for the reason you proposed, and not as a dispatcher for every business. Plenty of needs recur more often than a household's one or two tradie jobs a year: calls to banks, telcos and Centrelink (an estimated 0.5–2 a month per adult), seven to twelve insurance, energy and telco renewal moments a year, after-hours health care, tech help for ageing parents and, among businesses, short-stay turnovers and venue equipment failures. Run through your own test, how often a user needs a new or currently available human they cannot already reach, the frequent consumer needs fail one by one: Apple and Google already wait on hold for free in Australia, 1800MEDICARE made after-hours GP care free in January 2026, credit and insurance referrals are the most heavily policed lead-generation market in the country, and the rest have no proven payer. "Businesses make more calls" holds mainly for calls to vendors they already use, which is why business-dispatch platforms charge providers a few dollars per invoice rather than A$15–40 per lead; the flip works instead because the person texting carries the cost of delay, and because roughly 100 paying venues close the arithmetic that otherwise needs 2,800–9,600 active households. The single recommended path is a business tool whose beachhead is independent café, restaurant and pub groups of 2–10 venues in a 3–5 km zone around East Melbourne, reached first through your Lightspeed integration, with a one-line offer: text one number, your own technician is tried first, and if nobody can call back within 15 minutes a vetted commercial technician will. Price it at about A$79 per venue per month for staffed hours with provider fees only on first connections to new venues, add short-stay co-hosts on the same roster from month six, and open a consumer number only after business fill holds 80% for four weeks. Kill or re-aim after a 90-day, 20-venue pilot if venues average fewer than 0.8 requests a month, fewer than 30% of requests need a vendor the venue cannot already reach, or fewer than 10 accounts pay. Every figure here comes from search-result summaries, much of the frequency data is American or vendor-supplied, and no customer has been interviewed, so the scores and frequencies below are labelled hypotheses that the pilot exists to test.

## Frequent consumer needs exist, but each is free, regulated or unpaid

Your framing is the right filter, and the leakage literature backs it: marketplaces lose repeat business whenever the same two parties transact again, as Homejoy did when only **15–20% of customers rebooked a cleaning within a month** ([University of Sydney](https://internet-policy-meco.sydney.edu.au/?p=3581)). Applied to consumers, the filter turns up several needs that beat household tradie frequency. None survives the second filter the research kept returning to: who pays when the human is reached, and what free option already exists.

**Calls to institutions are the most frequent need, and the waiting is already free.** Australians spent an estimated **123 million hours on hold in 2024, about 11.1 hours each**, in a vendor survey that probably over-represents frequent callers ([ServiceNow](https://newsroom.servicenow.com/press-releases/details/2025/Australian-wait-times-soar-to-123-million-hours-on-hold/default.aspx)). Services Australia alone handled **44.3 million calls in 2024–25** ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-10/annual-report-2024-25.pdf)), about 1.6 per resident, which together with telco, bank, energy and insurer contacts supports an estimate of 0.5–2 institutional calls a month per adult. The pain is uneven and falling. Across the industry **91.3% of calls are answered within 10 minutes, at an average of 1 minute 49 seconds**, with banks the slowest sector at more than three times the others' waits ([ACXPA](https://acxpa.com.au/call-centre-hold-times-in-australia/); [ACXPA](https://acxpa.com.au/glossary/average-speed-of-answer/)), and in one year Services Australia cut congestion messages by 85.5%, answered calls 22% faster and offered 1.6 million free callbacks, 58% of them accepted ([transparency.gov.au](https://www.transparency.gov.au/publications/social-services/services_australia/services-australia-annual-report-2024-25/part-2-%E2%80%93-our-performance/2.1-annual-performance-statements-2024%E2%80%9325); [Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-10/annual-report-2024-25.pdf)). Phones now do the waiting: pre-release coverage of iOS 26 Hold Assist lists English (Australia), though one source omits Australia ([Appleosophy](https://appleosophy.com/2025/06/09/apple-brings-call-screening-hold-assist-and-live-translation-to-ios-26/); [iGeeksBlog](https://www.igeeksblog.com/iphone-hold-assist-in-ios-26/)), and Pixel's Hold for Me is officially live here ([Google](https://support.google.com/phoneapp/answer/10071878)). Google's fully agentic Gemini Call for Me navigates menus and holds, but only for US Pixel 11 owners calling US numbers ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/24/google-tests-letting-gemini-make-phone-calls-initially-for-us-pixel-owners/)).

Two structural problems remain even where the gap is open. Services Australia and the ATO verify callers by voice biometrics, so the user must be bridged in live rather than represented ([Australian Government Architecture](https://architecture.digital.gov.au/services-australia-voice-biometric-solution)); the mechanic is "we ring them and conference you in", not "the right human calls you". And the real private-sector pain is resolution, not reach: "no or delayed action by provider" is the TIO's top complaint ([WhistleOut](https://www.whistleout.com.au/Broadband/News/tio-report-november-2024)). Pine raised a **US$25M Series A** by charging for dollar outcomes such as lower bills ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251203384902/en)), not for minutes saved. Hold-for-me is a good retention feature for any product built on this stack and a weak standalone wedge; the first-round researcher's judgement scores gave it 3/10 for willingness to pay and 2/10 for defensibility against Apple and Google.

**Health has the highest raw frequency and the worst economics.** Medicare funded **167.2 million GP attendances in 2024–25** ([AIHW](https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/primary-health-care/general-practice-allied-health-primary-care)), about six per person, but nearly all go to a regular GP. The low-leakage slice is small, with 5.4% of people using an after-hours GP and 8.8% urgent GP care in a year ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/media-centre/media-releases/more-people-waiting-longer-see-gps-urgent-medical-care)), and government then took it: 1800MEDICARE replaced healthdirect's after-hours service with free nurse triage and free after-hours GP consults from 1 January 2026, a date reported by the RACGP's newsGP and a launch confirmed in this research only by a vendor press release ([newsGP](https://www1.racgp.org.au/newsgp/professional/pledge-to-boost-gp-access-in-after-hours-telehealt); [Montu](https://www.montu.com.au/press-release/montu-welcomes-launch-of-1800-medicare)), and the free Medicare Mental Health Check In website went live the same day ([Department of Health](https://www.health.gov.au/news/free-online-mental-health-service-now-available)). The business model is closed too. Inciting a practitioner to unprofessional conduct, which includes paying for referrals, is an offence under s136 of the National Law with reported maximum fines of **A$60,000 for individuals and A$120,000 for companies** ([Medical Republic](https://www.medicalrepublic.com.au/?p=129373)), so the tradie-style per-lead fee is unusable for any AHPRA-registered profession.

Pets are the best health-adjacent fit. With **73% of households owning a pet** ([Animal Medicines Australia](https://animalmedicinesaustralia.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/250918-Animal-Medicines-Australias-Pets-in-Australia-report-show-nearly-three-quarters-of-Australian-homes-now-have-a-pet.pdf)), tele-vet consults at A$78.50 standard and A$165.60 after hours ([NT Vet](https://ntvet.com.au/remote-services.html)) and emergency visits starting at A$300–400 ([Choosi](https://www.choosi.com.au/pet-insurance/articles/cost-of-vet-visit-2025)), "tell me whether to drive to the emergency vet" is availability-driven and owner-paid. Against it, Medibank and Pet Insurance Australia already give policyholders up to four free Vetchat consults a month ([Medibank](https://www.medibank.com.au/pet-insurance/why-medibank-pet-insurance/)), no data supports any estimate of how often a household has an urgent pet problem, and Victoria's vet board allows telemedicine only where a vet–client relationship already exists ([VPRBV Guideline 13](https://vetboard.vic.gov.au/VPRBV/VPRBV_Guidelines/Guideline_13_-_Telemedicine_in_the_provision_of_veterinary_services.aspx)), a direct constraint on a Melbourne launch. Last-minute babysitting and same-day help for an ageing parent are real immediacy gaps, but each needs dense screened supply in every suburb, and the good match then leaks to direct booking, the Homejoy pattern again.

**Quote shopping has the money and the regulators.** A household faces an estimated seven to twelve renewal or switching moments a year but acts on few: **78.4% of general insurance policies are renewed without approaching another insurer and 4.2% switch** ([Mozo](https://mozo.com.au/insurance/car-insurance/articles/why-aren-t-aussies-comparing-insurance-companies-at-renewal-time)), and ASIC found 68% of car insurance customers never contact their insurer at renewal even though 31% of those who do get a lower premium ([ASIC](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-189mr-consumers-left-in-the-dark-about-rising-car-insurance-premiums-asic-warns)). That puts realised shopping at roughly 0.1–0.25 events a month per household (estimate), rising only if a service prompts each renewal. The richest leg is credit, where a broker earns 0.55–0.70% upfront, **A$3,300–4,200 on a A$600,000 loan** ([Hunter Galloway](https://www.huntergalloway.com.au/mortgage-broker-fees-australia-2026-guide-for-first-home-buyers/)), but it is closed to a solo founder on every side. The consumer-credit referrer exemption does not cover a business whose main activity is passing on leads ([MFAA](https://portal.mfaa.com.au/broker-toolbox/legal-and-compliance/nccp-bid/Referrer%20exemption%20File); [Brightlaw](https://www.brightlaw.com.au/new-credit-licensing-exemptions-for-referrers-and-temporary-employees/)); ASIC's list of lead-generation entities reached **63 by June 2026** ([ASIC](https://asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-125mr-asic-expands-list-of-known-entities-involved-in-lead-generation/); [Financial Newswire](https://financialnewswire.com.au/financial-planning/asic-adds-19-entities-to-lead-gen-list/)); ASIC sued an alleged lead-auction "comparison" operator in September 2026 ([ASIC](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-221mr-asic-sues-lead-generation-business-over-alleged-misleading-comparison-claims/)); the ACCC wants digital lead generation brought inside the unsolicited-selling rules ([ACCC](https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/misleading-and-high-pressure-unsolicited-sales-practices-widespread)); and a general unfair-trading prohibition with new subscription rules starts on **1 July 2027** ([Allens](https://www.allens.com.au/insights-news/insights/2026/07/australias-new-unfair-trading-practices-regime-what-businesses-need-to-know/)). The useful finding is that a consented, masked, one-provider callback is the opposite of the conduct regulators are attacking, which makes it attractive to a licensed partner later rather than a launch category now.

Expert advice fails differently. JustAnswer-style breadth needs thousands of experts, its subscription capture drew an FTC suit in January 2026 over US$1–5 "join" offers that became US$28–125 monthly plans ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/327857)), and most expert fields (accountants, vets, mechanics) turn into sticky relationships after the first find. The house hunt is genuinely high-frequency and low-leakage, because every listing has a different selling agent to chase, but the burst lasts three to six months and recurs only every several years.

**Tech help for older parents is the strongest consumer candidate and the least evidenced.** The adult child is both the natural texter ("get someone to call Mum about her iPhone") and the payer, regulation is light, and local in-home help costs about A$120 for a first hour ([ACT Seniors Card directory](https://www.actseniorscard.org.au/index.php/act-seniors-card-discount-directory/category/computers-and-computer-support-2)) against free but slow programs such as Be Connected and council sessions ([City of Melbourne](https://www.melbourne.vic.gov.au/community/health-support-services/for-older-people/Pages/help-technology-internet.aspx)). The quotes researcher ranked it first at 39/60 but rated the evidence low to medium, because no data exists on how often older Australians need help or whether families will pay monthly. It also does not reuse the tradie roster, since the supply is remote helpers, and it leaks heavily once one friendly local helper is introduced. It is a credible later household extension, not the path.

**Precedent explains why frequency alone never rescues a consumer access product.** Measured use of reach-a-human services is low even when prepaid: Teladoc reported **9.4% annual utilisation** in 2018 ([Motley Fool transcript](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2019/02/28/teladoc-tdoc-q4-2018-earnings-conference-call-tran.aspx)), an employer concierge at 40% uptake generated about 4,000 requests a year across 10,000 employees ([Workforce](https://workforce.com/news/an-intriguing-development-for-workplace-concierge-services)), and healthdirect's roughly 1.3 million calls a year ([healthdirect](https://about.healthdirect.gov.au/resources/news/healthdirect-expands-virtual-care-pathways)) come to about 0.05 per Australian. The 2015 text-a-number concierges proved demand and then lost money: Magic lost money on a quarter of its orders ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=1257663)), GoButler narrowed to flights ([Deutsche Startups](https://www.deutsche-startups.de/2016/03/21/gobutler-ist-kein-full-service-butler-mehr/)), and Facebook M reached about 2,000 people before closing ([Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/social-media/facebook-m-beta-discontinued/)). The human-connection services that lasted were paid per member by a sponsor, as Medicare Advantage plans pay Papa ([KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/news/article/medicare-advantage-plans-senior-companions-profits/)), or moved from consumers to a business channel, as Hello Alfred did through landlords because developer deals cost less than acquiring households one by one ([Harvard D3](https://d3.harvard.edu/platform-digit/submission/hello-alfred-automating-the-on-demand-economy/index.html)).

## Business frequency multiplies only across sites, trades and urgent gaps

For a business, your metric becomes a product of four terms: sites per account, trade categories per site, the incident rate, and the share of incidents the usual vendor cannot cover in time. Only operators of several revenue-critical sites score high on all four, which is why the second, deeper research round narrowed the field sharply. Where it disagreed with the first round, its findings replace the first round's in three places: a smaller short-stay market with lower per-listing prices, provider fees in business dispatch capped at a few dollars per invoice, and a crowd of AI dispatch tools already present in short-stay and property management.

**Short-stay co-hosts have the most urgent needs per account, in a small and contested pool.** The second round estimates **6–16 urgent events a month for a 15-listing Melbourne operator, of which perhaps 2–8 need a vendor outside the co-host's own roster**, lowering the first round's 2–3 such needs a week; the estimate is fragile because it rests on a cleaner no-show rate somewhere between a 3% assumption and an unverified 12% figure attributed to Turno. The pain is real: **34.3% of 554 mostly US hosts and managers lost bookings or reviews to staffing or contractor problems in 2025**, and nearly 40% struggle to find dependable cleaners, in a vendor survey ([Short Term Rentalz](https://shorttermrentalz.com/news/new-hospitable-data-reveals-causes-of-booking-losses/)). But the buyer pool is small: only 5.18% of Melbourne Airbnb hosts manage 6–20 listings and 2.04% more than 20 ([Hospitable](https://hospitable.com/melbourne-rental-market)), about 650–1,500 multi-listing operators (estimate), in a market whose active listings fell 5.3% to 33,321 in the year to June 2026 ([AirDNA](https://www.airdna.co/vacation-rental-data/app/au/victoria/melbourne/supply)). Prices are lower than the first round assumed, because hosts pay Turno **US$8–10 per property per month just to coordinate their own cleaners** ([Turno](https://turno.com/pricing/)) and Breezeway US$6–19.99 ([PricingSaaS](https://pricingsaas.com/companies/breezeway)), so a typical 10–15-listing account is worth about A$3–6k a year rather than A$5–9k. And the gap has narrowed: Trellis (Y Combinator) builds agents that dispatch cleaners and maintenance staff ([Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/Q15-trellis-autonomous-agents-for-short-term-rentals)), Hospitable runs a marketplace of more than 150,000 cleaners ([Short Term Rentalz](https://shorttermrentalz.com/news/new-hospitable-data-reveals-causes-of-booking-losses/)), and Hostaway ships an AI co-host ([Short Term Rentalz](https://shorttermrentalz.com/news/hostaway-launches-ai-assistant/)), although none promises a human on the phone within 15 minutes or has proven Australian supply.

**Independent hospitality venues have fewer needs per site but far more value per incident.** The only hard frequency data is American and chain-skewed: operators surveyed by 86 Repairs in 2020 reported a **critical piece of equipment failing about 1.5 times a month per location** ([QSRweb](https://www.qsrweb.com/news/restaurants-endure-at-least-15-monthly-equipment-issues/)), and ServiceChannel counted more than 1 million work orders across 16,441 quick-service locations over 2022–2024 ([ServiceChannel](https://servicechannel.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/ServiceChannel-Benchmark-Report-2025-R5-DIGITAL.pdf)). One Melbourne coffee-machine service firm handles 40–50 jobs a day, rising to 80 at busy times ([BeanScene](https://www.beanscenemag.com.au/coffee-technicians-the-industrys-unsung-heroes/)). With less kit than a chain store, an independent generates an estimated **0.5–1.5 urgent calls a month per café and 1–3 per restaurant or pub**. Downtime is what makes each call matter. US operators report average critical-equipment downtime of six hours, with 42% citing a day or more, at about **US$615 lost per day** ([Facilitator](https://facilitator.mydigitalpublication.com/articles/streamlining-success)); Melbourne commercial work already carries a **A$148.50 diagnostic fee** and an average coffee-machine repair of about A$375 ([Service Sphere](https://www.servicesphere.com.au/FAQ)), and after-hours electrical call-outs cost A$300–500 ([Approved Electrix](https://www.approvedelectrix.com.au/?p=14765)). Café sale listings imply A$15–36k of weekly takings, so a four-hour Saturday-morning espresso outage plausibly costs A$1,000–2,500 in sales (estimate). Funded US players prove buyers pay for this: 86 Repairs has raised about US$24M including a US$15.2M Series A ([FER](https://www.fermag.com/articles/86-repairs-secures-15-2m-in-series-a-funding/)), ResQ a US$39M Series A ([BetaKit](https://betakit.com/?p=314107)) and Prefix a US$7.5M seed in April 2026 ([VC News Daily](https://vcnewsdaily.com/prefix/venture-capital-funding/qghvrpmkks)), all aimed at chains and none found operating in Australia.

**The remaining business segments are crowded or closed.** Property managers have volume, about 3.3 maintenance requests per unit per year on US data ([Hemlane](https://hemlane.com/resources/rental-property-maintenance-requests/)), but Tapi says it powers maintenance for more than 450,000 properties across Australia and New Zealand ([Tapi](https://tapihq.com/about)), a figure a PropertyMe page puts at 160,000 properties across 300 agencies ([PropertyMe](https://www.propertyme.com.au/blog/propertyme/integrator-spotlight-tapi)); Bricks and Agent matches jobs to more than 50,000 hipages trades ([iTWire](https://itwire.com/business-it-news/data/proptech-startup-bricks-and-agent-using-ai-to-help-tenants-and-property-managers-alike)); Y Combinator's 2026 cohorts include AI maintenance coordinators such as CentralComs ([NeuronFeed](https://neuronfeed.com/startups/centralcoms)); and owners pay for repairs, so agencies are a supply partner, not a buyer. Sales agents' pre-sale fix-ups share handyman, painter and cleaner supply and have no trades tool, since Realhub covers marketing suppliers ([Domain](https://agent.domain.com.au/residential/realhub/)), but their deadlines run in days rather than minutes and Victorian agents must pass supplier rebates to the vendor ([Consumer Affairs Victoria](https://www.consumer.vic.gov.au/licensing-and-registration/estate-agents/running-your-business/authorities-commissions-and-contracts/authorities-rebates-and-commission)), which suits a third segment. Last-minute shift staffing has the highest raw frequency of any segment and is closed: Victoria's labour-hire definition from 1 October 2026 captures "bare intermediaries" supplying labour anywhere in a supply chain ([Labour Hire Authority](https://www.labourhireauthority.vic.gov.au/latest-news/final-changes-to-labour-hire-law-to-take-effect-from-october-2026/)), with penalties above **A$660,000** for hosts using unlicensed providers reported by a single trade source ([Staffing Industry Analysts](https://www.staffingindustry.com/news/global-daily-news/victoria-tightens-labour-hire-licensing-rules-for-supply-chains)). Pharmacy locums already fill in a self-reported median of 16 minutes on Locumate ([LinkedIn](https://au.linkedin.com/in/surgesingh)), and construction labour carries both labour-hire and workplace-safety load.

**The broad version of the flip fails the frequency test outright.** Of 2.81 million actively trading businesses, only **996,203 employ anyone** ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/business-indicators/counts-australian-businesses-including-entries-and-exits/jul2022-jun2026)), and no Australian survey measures how often small businesses seek a new provider. The proxies point to lumpy, low rates. Only 19% of small businesses switched insurer each year in 2017 despite widespread dissatisfaction ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/financial-services/our-insights/sme-insurance-in-australia-a-market-ripe-for-change)), accountants are long relationships, and most supplier time goes on quoting, ordering and chasing existing suppliers, the admin load that 62% of owners say takes more of their week than the work itself in research commissioned by OpenAI and CommBank ([Dynamic Business](https://dynamicbusiness.com/news/commbank-and-openai-are-betting-ai-can-fix-the-tradie-admin-problem)). A typical employing small business plausibly runs 3–10 new-provider searches a year (speculative estimate), barely above a household. The categories that pay most, business finance and insurance, are the least frequent or need a licensed partner, and multi-category lead marketplaces are struggling: Bark rates 3.2 from 676 ProductReview reviews amid provider complaints about shared and stale leads ([ProductReview](https://www.productreview.com.au/listings/barkcom)), and Airtasker closed Oneflare on 30 June 2026 ([Oneflare](https://help.oneflare.com.au/hc/en-au/articles/58074479988377-Oneflare-is-closing-here-s-what-you-need-to-know)).

## Businesses do call more, mostly to vendors they already have

Your claim has three parts, and the evidence treats them differently. "Businesses make more calls" is true for operators of many revenue-critical sites and false for the typical small business, as above. Even where it holds, the extra calls mostly go to vendors the business already knows, and that matters most for the plan you already have, whose provider pays A$15–40 per connected call. Business dispatch has no precedent for that fee. ServiceChannel, which Fortive bought for about **US$1.2B** ([Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/fortive-ftv-buying-servicechannel-for-about-1-2-billion)), charges providers **1.5% of each approved invoice, capped at US$4** ([ServiceChannel](https://servicechannel.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/SCUCP/pages/586088951/ServiceChannel+Usage+Fees)), and Australia's OurTradie charges tradies **2%, capped at A$20 plus GST** ([OurTradie](https://support.ourproperty.com.au/hc/en-au/articles/7760718663193-OurTradie-FAQs)). Business buyers bring their own vendors, so the money comes from the buyer as a per-site subscription, and a new-vendor fee becomes a secondary line that shrinks as vendors get saved. The third part follows: flipping turns the product from matchmaking into vendor coordination with a backup network, and changes who pays.

**That change is also the flip's strength, because it dissolves leakage instead of fighting it.** A venue that routes even its saved technician through the line under a flat per-site fee saves nothing by going around it, which is why Turno can already charge hosts per property to coordinate cleaners they found themselves ([Turno](https://turno.com/pricing/)). The network becomes the reason to keep paying, because own vendors fail exactly when urgency peaks. The arithmetic follows. The build plan modelled about 700–800 fulfilled requests a month to cover fixed costs and an A$10,000 monthly founder draw; at the assumed one to three tradie needs per household per year, that needs roughly **2,800–9,600 active households** in one city, acquired at an assumed A$30–150 each with a one-to-seven-year payback on provider fees (red-team estimates). On the business side, **about 100 paying venues at A$99 a month, or 130 at A$79**, plus early new-vendor fees covers the same draw (this report's estimate, using the build plan's modelled A$2.3–2.9 cost per request), which is under 1% of an estimated 17–20k serviceable Melbourne venues.

Business demand is also predictable from trading hours and turnover calendars, which is what lets a 15-minute promise be staffed, and it suits the marketplace advice to seed the harder side first in a tiny network ([RaftLabs summary of Andrew Chen](https://www.raftlabs.com/blog/two-sided-marketplace-cold-start)); here the harder side is responsive local supply, and a cluster of business accounts is the cheapest way to build it. Every precedent that switched sides moved from consumers toward businesses or sponsors, never the reverse. In Australia, Airtasker's membership, launched in February 2026 at about A$849–896 a year (sources differ) to remove connection fees, passed **10,000 paying members by 30 June** ([What's The Damage](https://whatsthedamage.com.au/tradies/hipages-alternatives/); [Airtasker](https://support.airtasker.com/hc/en-au/articles/61104692235033-Airtasker-Business-Membership-Terms-Conditions)), a sign that frequent buyers prepay to avoid per-use fees.

**The flip goes wrong in three places.** Breadth is the first: a horizontal "dispatcher for all businesses" repeats Managed by Q, which WeWork bought for US$220M in 2019 and sold to Eden for about **US$25M** eleven months later ([The Real Deal](https://therealdeal.com/new-york/2020/03/03/wework-sells-managed-by-q-to-rival-startup-at-90-discount/)). The channel is the second: businesses do not adopt a text number for its own sake, and in a vendor-sponsored survey 64% of US small businesses could receive texts but only 13% used them with customers ([Small Business Trends](https://smallbiztrends.com/2024/12/text-message-marketing-statistics.html)), so the adoption driver has to be a named outcome, a qualified technician calling within 15 minutes or the month credited. The third is the role of the human call. For a venue the call is a means, a remote triage that often resolves a tripped breaker or narrows the fault before anyone drives over, and the business pays for the outcome. Your belief in human-to-human connection survives the flip, but the product sells the guarantee and records the connection rather than selling the connection itself.

## Venue groups beat co-hosts for this founder, narrowly

The deeper round put two segments on top, and they are close. The short-stay researcher kept co-hosts first at 43/70, the hospitality researcher scored independent venue groups 50/80 "for this founder", and the red team recommended leading with co-hosts for supply overlap with a later consumer product while running venues alongside for founder fit. A solo founder cannot run two beachheads at once, so this report picks one on the comparison below, which is judgement mixing cited facts and labelled estimates.

| Factor | Independent venue groups | Short-stay co-hosts | Edge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Urgent needs per typical account | About 2–6 a month for a 3-venue group (estimate) | About 6–16 a month for 15 listings (estimate, fragile) | Co-hosts |
| Value per incident | A$1,000–2,500 lost sales per weekend-morning outage (estimate) | About A$550 per lost booking at an A$185 nightly rate (estimate) | Venues |
| Price anchor | No Australian comparable; US managed repair desks; A$79–149 per venue (hypothesis) | Turno US$8–10, Breezeway US$6–19.99 per listing | Venues |
| Melbourne buyer pool | About 17–20k serviceable venues (estimate); groups a minority | About 650–1,500 multi-listing operators (estimate); supply down 5.3% | Venues |
| Local competition | None found serving independents | Trellis, TIDY, Hospitable, Turno, Hostaway (mostly US supply) | Venues |
| Founder's own edge | Lightspeed integration: profiles, downtime ROI, warm leads | None specific | Venues |
| Overlap with a later consumer roster | Partial: plumbing, electrical, locks, glass | High: cleaners, locksmiths, handymen, plumbers | Co-hosts |
| Ease of listing buyers | Walk-ins, Lightspeed, roasters, AHA Victoria | Public Airbnb co-host pages, host groups | Co-hosts |
| Structural churn | 12.03% annual closures | Levy, owners-corporation bans, platform fees | Even |

Venue groups win five of the nine rows, co-hosts three, and one is even. **The decisive rows are the ones where you hold an advantage no other founder has.** Your Lightspeed integration turns onboarding into a pre-filled venue profile and every outage into a measured dollar loss; Lightspeed says more than **10,000 venues** in Australia and New Zealand use it, a marketing figure ([Lightspeed](https://www.lightspeedhq.com/au/lightspeed-vs-square/)), and lists more than 95 integration partners with none observed in maintenance ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1486190847)); and no Australian competitor serves independents. The integration's limits matter too. Point-of-sale data is a weak failure detector, because a dead espresso machine shows in sales only after 20–40 minutes and a failed cool room never shows at all, which is the job of sensors such as SafetyCulture's ([SafetyCulture](https://safetyculture.com/monitoring/refrigeration-monitoring)); its value is onboarding, proof of return and warm introductions. Co-hosts' frequency advantage, meanwhile, rests on an unverified no-show rate, their price is anchored by US software at US$6–20 per listing, and software entrants are closing their gap. Because the call is narrow, the pilot is built so that a frequency failure in venues redirects to co-hosts on the same roster rather than ending the business.

**The beachhead is independent café, restaurant and pub groups with 2–10 venues inside a 3–5 km zone around East Melbourne**, covering the CBD, Fitzroy, Collingwood, Carlton, Richmond and Southbank. Recruit first among venues already using your Lightspeed integration and then other Lightspeed venues, next by walking the café strips, then through two or three coffee roasters as design partners, and through AHA Victoria's partnership program, which advertises access to more than 1,000 member pubs and hotels ([AHA Vic](https://www.ahavic.com.au/partnership-benefits/)). Groups come first because the owner is the dispatcher and often off-site, and because single cafés close fast: **12.03% of cafés, restaurants and takeaways closed in the year to July 2026**, against 6.69% across all industries ([CreditorWatch](https://creditorwatch.com.au/blog/one-in-eight-cafes-restaurants-closing)). The same zone overlaps Melbourne's densest commercial short-stay listings, which cluster in Central Melbourne, Docklands, Southbank, Fitzroy and St Kilda ([AHURI](https://www.ahuri.edu.au/research/news/tourists-turf-out-tenants-airbnb-hotspots)), so the second segment can largely reuse the first segment's roster without moving.

**The offer is one line: text one number; your own technician is tried first; if they cannot call back within 15 minutes, a vetted commercial technician will.** Four features make it more than a phone tree. Coffee faults go to the roaster's technician first, since roasters bundle technical support with wholesale coffee ([Five Senses](https://fivesenses.com.au/wholesale-coffee)), and the network earns its keep on the long tail of refrigeration, warewashers, plumbing and drains, electrical, gas, locks, glass and exhaust. Landlord routing sends a timestamped written notice when the fault is the landlord's, because section 52 of Victoria's Retail Leases Act makes landlords maintain the structure, fixtures and landlord-owned plant whatever the lease says ([VSBC](https://www.vsbc.vic.gov.au/who-we-help/retail-tenants-landlords/repairs-maintenance/)). An asset and warranty register builds itself from conversations, the job 86 Repairs does on every incident ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/compare/86-repairs-vs-resq)). And a monthly downtime report from Lightspeed compares sales during each outage with the venue's baseline, so renewal is argued in dollars. The 15-minute promise is a triage phone call, not arrival on site, and it runs in staffed hours of 7am–10pm, seven days, with an after-hours tier added only once the roster supports it; maintenance coordinators such as Lula sell after-hours and 24/7 cover as their premium tier ([Lula](https://lula.life/how-it-works)).

**Pricing follows business-dispatch precedent rather than your consumer plan, and every price here is an untested hypothesis drawn from adjacent products.** The venue pays about A$79 per venue per month for the staffed-hours tier and A$129–149 for 24/7 once it exists, billed monthly with no lock-in, because hospitality buyers resist contracts and the 2027 unfair-trading rules target hard-to-cancel subscriptions ([Allens](https://www.allens.com.au/insights-news/insights/2026/07/australias-new-unfair-trading-practices-regime-what-businesses-need-to-know/)). A per-incident fallback of about A$29–49 serves single-site cafés that will not subscribe. Saved vendors are routed free. A provider fee of about A$20–60 applies only to a technician's first connection to a new venue, priced against hipages leads that cost up to about A$200 and are shared with up to three businesses ([What's The Damage](https://whatsthedamage.com.au/tradies/hipages-cost/)); budget it at A$10–40 per venue per month in year one, falling as vendors get saved. An SLA credit applies whenever no qualified human calls within 15 minutes in staffed hours.

On those prices, a Melbourne-only business reaches a founder salary at about 100–130 venues and, on the hospitality researcher's estimates, tops out between about **A$0.6M of annual recurring revenue at 3%** of the city's roughly 13,000 sit-down venues and **A$1.3M at 5%** including pubs and food retail, with SaaS churn of 3–7% of small-business customers a month as the benchmark to beat ([Fungies](https://fungies.io/saas-metrics-guide-nrr-ltv-cac-cohort-analysis-2026/)). That is a solid bootstrapped business, not yet a venture-scale one; scale needs other Australian cities, Lightspeed markets abroad or the consumer channel. Customer acquisition cost has to stay under about A$900–2,700 per account to hold payback near the nine months typical for contracts under US$5k a year ([SaaS Hero](https://www.saashero.net/?p=23807)), which founder-led walk-ins can achieve and paid acquisition cannot.

## The build plan carries over, with the masked call repurposed

Almost none of the build plan is wasted, because a venue repair request is the same timed dispatch job: intake, triage, ranking, fan-out, a race for the first acceptance, connection and confirmation. The core carries over as designed. One durable Vercel Workflow run handles each request; Supabase Postgres in Sydney holds the twelve-table schema of users, jobs, job events, providers, provider stats, outreach attempts, consents, phone numbers, masking sessions, call records, charges and a cost ledger; Haiku 5.5 extracts the job while code decides scope; SMS job cards go from sticky 04 numbers to an opted-in roster before any call; a scripted outreach call reaches non-responders; the consent ledger holds Spam Act and Telemarketing Standard evidence; and the founder console keeps its takeover switch. The modelled cost stays at about A$2.3–2.9 per request, and the plan's split between "now" and "quote" jobs maps directly onto venues, where equipment failures are "now" jobs and refits and planned servicing are quotes.

**Four additions turn it into a business tool.** The first is an account and site layer, with each venue's name, address, trading hours and group structure pre-filled from your Lightspeed sync and an asset table recording equipment, brand and model, warranty status and whether the landlord owns it. The second is a saved-vendor branch before the ranking step: the workflow texts and then rings the venue's own technician with a 10–15 minute timeout and falls through to the roster only if nobody accepts, which is the mechanism that converts leakage into retention. The third moves billing from per-connection charges to subscriptions plus new-vendor fees, with SLA credits computed from the same call records. The fourth adds the landlord-notice template and the Lightspeed downtime report. Your existing Lightspeed integration can stay wherever it runs today and feed venue profiles and sales baselines into the new system; the build plan's reasons for keeping the dispatch record in Postgres rather than Firestore (ranking queries, an append-only ledger and charge reconciliation) are unchanged.

**The masked callback stays, but its job changes.** In the consumer plan it protected the customer's number and produced billing evidence for a per-connection fee. Businesses want the vendor relationship, so masking adds little privacy value; it now timestamps the first callback from a new network technician, which is the evidence behind the SLA credit and the response score that ranks the roster, and the venue can save the vendor for direct contact afterwards. The build-it-yourself design is still required because Twilio has closed its Proxy product to new customers ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/docs/proxy/understanding-phone-number-management)), and the cheap local 02 proxies still matter because receiving a call on an Australian mobile number costs US$0.05 a minute against US$0.01 on a local number ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/voice/pricing/au)). The roster keeps its consumer launch categories of plumbing and drains, electrical, locksmiths and handymen, which make up much of a venue's long tail and nearly all of a co-host's needs, and adds commercial refrigeration, warewasher and coffee-machine technicians, gas fitters and glaziers, with plumbers and gas fitters checked against Victoria's Building and Plumbing Commission register ([BPC](https://www.bpc.vic.gov.au/find-and-check-a-practitioner)). Roster consent must cover the same 7am–10pm, seven-day window the venue tier promises. One risk shrinks: the build plan's biggest open question, whether Linq can supply +61 iMessage lines, matters less, because a business account can be onboarded to a plain Australian SMS number and a saved contact card, whereas consumers find the service by the number itself.

## Ninety days, twenty venues, six gates

The sequence keeps one segment live at a time and lets metrics, not enthusiasm, open the next.

| Months | What runs | Gate to move on |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | Venue groups in the East Melbourne zone; manual, then automated dispatch | Day-90 gates below |
| 3–6 | Scale to 100–130 venues; roaster design partners; AHA Victoria pubs; Lightspeed marketplace listing (terms unchecked) | At least 80% fill for four straight weeks per trade (build-plan expansion gate) |
| 6–9 | Add co-hosts with 5–50 listings in the same zone at about A$15–20 per listing plus an urgent fee | At least 1 network-fallback event per 10 listings a month; a third of pilots paying A$15 or more per listing |
| 9–12 | Sales agents' pre-sale fix-ups; 24/7 tier | Roster providers opted in to after-hours work at after-hours rates |
| 12+ | Consumer number in the same zone under a separate brand, as overflow for roster tradies; then a second city | Business fill at 80% or more for four weeks |

Days 1–30 are discovery and supply. Run 15 owner interviews (five single-site cafés, five groups and five pubs) that measure urgent incidents a month, the share regular vendors cannot handle in time and the reaction to A$79–149; make ten calls to commercial refrigeration, coffee-machine and warewasher technicians to test whether they will commit to 15-minute callbacks and pay for new venues; recruit three to five committed providers per core trade in the zone; and hold two roaster conversations. Days 31–60 are a concierge pilot with 20 venues, ideally six to eight groups, free for the month, with saved vendors imported and you dispatching from the console. Days 61–90 convert pilots to paid plans and switch on automated fan-out and masking. The pass bars below combine the hospitality researcher's and red team's thresholds, which are self-set heuristics rather than benchmarks; the failure responses are this report's.

| Gate | Pass bar at day 90 | If it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | At least 0.8 requests per venue per month | If fill and payment pass, re-aim at co-hosts on the same roster for 60 days |
| Need beyond reach | At least 30% of requests need a non-saved vendor or an after-hours response | The product is coordination software only; sell it as a Lightspeed add-on or stop |
| Fill | At least 70% of staffed-hours requests get a qualified callback within 15 minutes; median 15 minutes or less | Narrow trades or hours; if still failing, the roster model does not hold |
| Willingness to pay | At least 10 paying accounts and half of piloted venues at A$79 or more | Test per-incident pricing once; kill below 5 paying accounts |
| Supply | At least 3 committed providers per core trade; roster acceptance of 60% or more | Drop that trade from the promise |
| Retention and load | At most 1 churned account a month; at most 40% of requests outside staffed hours | Above 40% after hours, pause sales until a 24/7 roster exists |

**Each failure has a defined meaning.** If frequency or need fails while supply and fill pass, the line works and the buyer is wrong, so co-hosts get 60 days on the same plumbers, locksmiths and handymen. If fill or supply fails, Melbourne's commercial trades cannot yet keep a 15-minute promise, and the fallback is the red team's partner play of selling overflow dispatch into the Lightspeed or Hostaway marketplaces or to small agencies without Tapi. If payment fails while usage holds, the per-incident tier gets one test before the business is killed. At any stage, five competitor moves should trigger a review: Google bringing Call for Me to Australia, Airtasker adding an urgent or callback flow or business seats on its membership, 86 Repairs, ResQ or Prefix entering Australia, a roaster, Ordermentum or Lightspeed adding a "get help" button, and Lightspeed changing its API terms.

## The evidence supports a test, not a verdict

All nine research notes, like those behind the build plan, worked from search-result summaries; every attempt to fetch a full page failed with a DNS error, so even official figures were seen only as snippets. The frequency evidence is the weakest link. The venue figure of 1.5 critical failures a month comes from a 2020 vendor survey of 250 mostly US chain operators, the co-host figures rest on a US-heavy vendor survey and an untraceable Turno statistic, the property-management rate is American, no Australian source measures how often small businesses seek new providers, and the household baseline of one to three tradie jobs a year is your own assumption. Market sizes are derived rather than counted, the 650–1,500 co-host accounts from undated vendor shares and the 17–20k serviceable venues from tourism data and a population ratio. Prices are comparables from adjacent products, not tested demand, and every score in the notes and in this report's tables is researcher judgement on scales that differ between notes (out of 40, 60, 70 and 80).

Several facts conflict between sources: Tapi's footprint (450,000 versus 160,000 properties), Airtasker's membership price (A$849 versus about A$896), whether Apple lists Australia for Hold Assist, Melbourne short-stay occupancy (55% to 71% depending on source), and 1800MEDICARE's launch, confirmed only by a vendor release. Nobody has been interviewed. The recommendation is therefore a ranked bet with explicit kill gates, and four results would overturn it: venues averaging fewer than about 0.4 requests a month, saved vendors handling more than about 85% of requests, specialist technicians refusing a 15-minute callback, or Lightspeed restricting API access. A Google launch of Call for Me in Australia would hurt the consumer path far more than this one, which is one more reason to start where the value lies in an account's history and guarantee rather than in the phone call.

## Conclusion

Your frequency metric needed a third term. Frequency times low leakage tells you how often someone needs a human they cannot reach; it says nothing about whether anyone pays at that moment. The consumer needs that score highest on the first two terms are exactly the ones governments, Apple and Google have made free or regulators have fenced off, while the business needs that score well on all three belong to owners who lose money by the minute and already pay A$148.50 just to have a fault diagnosed. Done narrowly, the flip also inverts the two problems the consumer plan spent most of its design on: leakage becomes the retention mechanism once saved vendors run through the same thread, and liquidity becomes tractable once demand arrives on trading-hour schedules in one zone.

The deeper implication is that the asset you would be building changes. The consumer plan's moat was a saved contact in thousands of phones and a roster that answers. The venue path adds a per-site record of equipment, vendors, warranties, landlord duties and downtime, the kind of buyer-side record that let ServiceChannel sell for nearly ten times revenue by one analyst's count ([Verdantix](https://www.verdantix.com/insights/blog/fortive-enhances-its-competitive-positioning-in-the-maintenance-software-market-through-its-1-2-billion-acquisition-of-servicechannel)), and one that no POS vendor, roaster or US repair platform holds for Melbourne's independents today. That record, and the response-scored roster it feeds, is also what would justify the consumer number later: tradies already taking venue jobs are the cheapest supply a consumer launch could ever recruit.
