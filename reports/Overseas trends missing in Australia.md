# Five automated services Australia still lacks

The clearest overseas trend that has not reached Australia is automation sold as a service: robots, wearable machines and unstaffed rooms that an operator owns and places in someone else's premises, then charges for by the serving, the hour or the month. Investors put **US$47.4B into physical-AI start-ups in the first half of 2026**, almost four times the previous half ([AI Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/physical-ai-startups-raised-474b-in-h1-quadrupling-h2-2025)), and Korea, China and Japan are filling with robot-run and unstaffed formats, yet in Australia nobody runs these service fleets, even where the machines are on sale. Five technology-led ideas survived three independent checks for any similar Australian business, all completed on 13 October 2026: **AI meal-assembly robots-as-a-service** for ready-meal, airline, hospital and aged-care kitchens; **exoskeleton hire for hikers and tourists**; **robotic massage placements**; **robotic manicure stations**; and **self-service IPL hair-removal studios**. Meal-assembly robots are the largest and most defensible prize but need an estimated A$2–10M and multi-year contracts, while exoskeleton hire is the cheapest and fastest test at an estimated A$0.55–1.3M, and the last three belong on one automated-wellness platform launched massage-first. Every idea carries a visible warning: US robotic-massage leader Aescape went through insolvency in 2026, Japan's self-service IPL chain ONESELF has slowed sharply, and robotic-manicure leader 10Beauty already holds an Australian trade mark. Verifiers rated absence at 65–85% confidence, not certainty, so a founder should claim only the narrow "first" set out in each section and should complete the pre-launch checks listed near the end.

The order reflects how big the prize is, how defensible it is, and how cheaply demand can be tested. **Meal-assembly robots rank first** because they sell multi-year contracts into an industry under structural labour pressure, and a fleet backed by local field engineers is hard to copy. Their weaknesses are the largest capital need, slow institutional buyers and the lowest first-check confidence (65%), which a dnata catering plant "semi-automated" since 2013 holds down. **Exoskeleton hire ranks second**: stock is now sold locally, a site run by a host on a revenue share breaks even at about four hires a day (estimate), and a pilot costs little. Ideas 3 to 5 each depend on a foreign hardware supplier with no Australian presence or, for IPL, on an insurer willing to cover customers who operate the device themselves, which makes each weaker alone and stronger as one platform.

## Overseas, automation is now sold by the serving, the hour and the month

Capital is pouring into machines that work in the physical world, and the business models gaining ground rent out what a machine does rather than selling the machine. Physical-AI start-ups raised **US$47.4B across 521 deals in H1 2026** ([AI Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/physical-ai-startups-raised-474b-in-h1-quadrupling-h2-2025)). Chef Robotics charges meal makers a fee below the cost of the labour its robots replace ([Chef Robotics](https://chefrobotics.ai/post/what-is-the-roi-of-chef-robots)). Serve Robotics ended 2025 with **2,000 delivery robots**, US$2.7M of revenue and a 2026 outlook of about US$26M ([Serve Robotics](https://investors.serverobotics.com/news-releases/news-release-details/serve-robotics-announces-fourth-quarter-and-full-year-2025)). Starship raised US$50M to grow its fleet from 2,700 to more than 12,000 robots by 2027 ([Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/starship-technologies-raises-50-million-us-robotics-delivery/802863/)), and WHILL's autonomous wheelchairs ran at 25 airports and facilities by April 2026 ([AviationPros](https://www.aviationpros.com/airport-business/news/55372613/whill-expands-autonomous-mobility-to-25-airports-worldwide)). Wearable robotics is following: exoskeleton maker Hypershell raised US$70M in November 2025 and another US$50M in May 2026 in a round led by Ant and Meituan ([DealStreetAsia](https://www.dealstreetasia.com/?p=464836); [Yicai](https://www2.yicaiglobal.com/news/chinese-exoskeleton-startup-hypershell-bags-usd50-million-in-new-round-led-by-ant-meituan)).

East Asia shows the same shift in shops and services. Korea had about **9,000 unstaffed stores** at the end of 2024, a count that roughly quadrupled between 2020 and 2025 ([The Star/Reuters](https://www.thestar.com.my/aseanplus/aseanplus-news/2026/07/02/south-korean-shops-turn-to-robots-self-service-to-escape-labour-woes)), and 32.6% of prospective Korean franchisees picked unmanned formats as the highest-growth sector for 2026 ([MTN](https://news.mtn.co.kr/news-detail/2026092116185852264)). China's unmanned-store sales topped ¥30bn in 2025, and **61.6% of its 24-hour convenience stores are staffed by day and "cloud-staffed" (remotely monitored) at night** ([Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/fortune/20260626/d0acfb8ad4dd4eee8ae8536f569748b4/c.html)). Japan's Lawson runs remote-avatar staff in 28 stores and estimates they save more than 1.5 staff-hours per store per day ([HR Grapevine](https://www.hrgrapevine.com/us/content/article/2025-01-02-japanese-store-pioneers-internationally-remote-digital-workers-in-store)). Europe and South Asia show that operated asset fleets can be profitable. Denmark's Freetrailer, which places unattended trailers at retailers' sites, earned DKK 144.1m revenue and DKK 26.9m EBIT in 2025 ([Freetrailer](https://freetrailer.com/en/2026/03/publishes-annual-report-for-july-to-december-2025/)), while in battery swapping India's Battery Smart passed 1,600 stations and Taiwan's Gogoro reached 665,000 subscribers ([Autocar Pro](https://www.autocarpro.in/news/battery-smart-secures-usd-13-million-debt-facility-from-responsability-133485?amp=1); [Gogoro](https://gogoro.gcs-web.com/news-releases/news-release-details/gogoro-releases-fourth-quarter-and-full-year-2025-financial)).

Australia receives software and hardware quickly; what it does not get is operated fleets. The list of rejected ideas below makes the pattern plain. US apps and marketplaces arrive within a year or two: Whatnot began hiring an Australian founding team in 2026 ([Redpoint](https://careers.redpoint.com/companies/whatnot/jobs/69398499-category-manager-australia)), Pinwheel's child-safe phones launched in April 2025 ([Pinwheel](https://blog.pinwheel.com/leading-child-safe-smartphone-pinwheel-launches-in-australia)) and Sniffspot already lists yards near Canberra ([Sniffspot](https://www.sniffspot.com/listings/nicholls-australian-capital-territory)). Local founders copy vertical AI fast, so restaurant phone ordering ([iTWire](https://itwire.com/it-industry-news/market/australia%E2%80%99s-%E2%80%98first-ai-ordering-telephone-agent%E2%80%99-launches)), dealership voice agents ([AI Directory](https://aidirectory.industry.gov.au/organisation/cfive-ai)) and AI-native commercial insurance broking ([BeInsure](https://beinsure.com/news/upcover-secured-19mn/)) all have Australian versions. Robots sold outright arrive too, from HP SitePrint layout robots ([Sprinter](https://www.sprinter.com.au/hp-siteprint-launches-in-australia)) to Carbon Robotics laser weeders ([Future Farming](https://www.futurefarming.com/crop-solutions/weed-pest-control/laser-weeding-arrives-in-australia-how-does-it-work/)) and ARENA-backed solar-construction robots ([ARENA](https://arena.gov.au/projects/luminous-robotics-scale-for-a-solar-future-deployment)).

What lags is the business that owns a fleet, holds local permits, employs field technicians and carries the liability. That needs an operator on the ground, and overseas leaders are busy at home. The final check found no 2026 Australian announcement from any leader in the five categories, only adjacent moves: Hypershell's retail launch, GMEX's cooking-robot order and 10Beauty's trade mark ([AAP](https://www.aap.com.au/aapreleases/cision20260520ae63922/); [AOL](https://lite.aol.com/tech/story/0022/20260323/9676662.htm); [IP Australia](https://search.ipaustralia.gov.au/trademarks/search/view/2551881/details)). Each of the five is a second-order business that became possible once a technology got cheap enough to rent out. Lighter consumer exoskeletons made hire possible, six-axis cobot packages selling for US$5,850–6,900 made massage bays affordable ([Alibaba](https://electronics.alibaba.com/product/dobot-robotics)), and vision AI let robots handle variable food.

## Idea 1: Meal-assembly robots rented for less than the labour they replace

### Chef Robotics proved robots-as-a-service across 119 million servings

The business installs AI-vision robot arms over a customer's existing conveyor line. Each robot recognises the tray in front of it and deposits a measured portion of rice, protein, vegetables or sauce, work now done by rows of people with scoops. The operator owns, maintains and upgrades the robots and charges a flat monthly fee per robot set below the cost of the labour displaced, so the customer avoids capital spending and integration risk ([Chef Robotics](https://chefrobotics.ai/solutions/robotics-as-a-service); [Chef Robotics](https://chefrobotics.ai/post/what-is-the-roi-of-chef-robots)).

Chef raised a **US$43.1M Series A** in 2025, of which US$22.5M was equipment financing for its fleet, when it had served 44M+ servings to customers including Amy's Kitchen and Sunbasket ([Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/chef-robotics-43-million-series-a-raised-for-ai-enabled-robotic-systems-for-meal-assembly); [Analytics India Mag](https://analyticsindiamag.com/ai-news-updates/chef-robotics-raises-43m-in-series-a-for-robotic-food-assembly)). It passed 100M servings across more than a dozen facilities in the US, Canada and Europe by April 2026 ([Hotel Online](https://www.hotel-online.com/news/chef-robotics-completes-100m-meal-servings-milestone)), and **119M+ servings across 15 manufacturers by 22 June 2026** ([Business Wire](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20260622708689/en/Chef-Robotics-Hires-Nicolas-De-Keijser-as-VP-of-Sales-to-Accelerate-Enterprise-Adoption-of-Physical-AI)). Its Chef+ system, sold only in the US, Canada and the UK, fills 30+ trays a minute against 15–20 for earlier units ([New Equipment Digest](https://newequipment.com/home/product/55314287/chef-robotics-food-robot-automates-meal-assembly-with-ai-and-multi-deposit)), and Chef has signed one of the world's largest airline caterers ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/sectors/technology/articles/chef-robotics-escaped-robot-cooking-161550763.html)). Partnerships with tray-sealer makers Proseal (JBT Marel) and Packline bundle the robots into complete lines ([Food Industry Executive](https://foodindustryexecutive.com/2025/10/chef-robotics-and-jbt-marels-proseal-partner-on-joint-solution-for-meal-assembly/); [Chef Robotics](https://chefrobotics.ai/post/chef-robotics-and-packline-partner-on-end-to-end-meal-production-automation)), and releases in 2026 added robot-to-robot coordination and tray assembly for meatpacking ([Robotics Tomorrow](https://www.roboticstomorrow.com/news/2026/03/12/introducing-robot-to-robot-communication-how-chef-robots-coordinate-to-maximize-throughput/26254/); [NuFFooDS Spectrum](https://nuffoodsspectrum.asia/2026/04/13/chef-robotics-unveils-ai-powered-tray-assembly-for-meatpacking.html)). Chef is not alone: Japan has Delibot and Foodly (about ¥8M a unit in 2020), and the Netherlands has Eatch, which assembles up to 5,000 meals a day for ISS in Amsterdam ([Business Insider Japan](https://www.businessinsider.jp/article/255224/); [Groovy Japan](https://groovyjapan.com/en/rt); [The Spoon](https://thespoon.tech/meet-the-dutch-robotic-kitchen-that-makes-five-thousand-meals-per-day/)).

The model is accelerating for three reasons. Vision systems now handle variable foods rather than only uniform items, renting robots turns a capital decision into an operating cost, and shortages of line workers have recast automation as a way to fill shifts rather than an industrial-relations fight. The caveats are real, though. Chef publishes neither its per-robot fee nor how many workers each robot replaces, so the Australian price below is derived from wage data rather than copied from a benchmark, and simpler restaurant robots rent for far less, about US$3k a month for Miso and US$1.5k for xRobotics ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/chat/h/d23e0776-0efe-4706-9410-b3548a83378b/); [PMQ](https://www.pmq.com/?p=52556)). The model also eats capital: Chef raised more equipment debt than equity in its Series A.

### Wage rises, shortages and new aged-care food rules set up the Australian buyer

Australia's ready-meal market reached about **A$2.83B in 2025** and is growing at roughly 6% a year ([GlobalData](https://www.globaldata.com/store/?p=1949640)), and prepared-meal manufacturing is a ~A$1.9bn industry of 246 businesses ([IBISWorld](https://www.ibisworld.com/australia/industry/prepared-meals-production/5478/)). An AFGC and Oxford Economics study published in May 2026 found that labourers make up 28% of the ~300,000-strong FMCG workforce, that around 10% of the sector's occupations are in shortage, and that the shortfall of packers will approach **5,000 by 2035** ([AFGC](https://afgc.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/OEA-AFGC-Workforce-pressures-Final-report-01052026.pdf); [Oxford Economics](https://www.oxfordeconomics.com/resource/australias-fmcg-workforce-challenge-is-becoming-structural/)). ANZ reports that food manufacturers are "increasingly turning to automation and AI" ([ANZ](https://www.anz.com/content/dam/anzcom/pdf/institutional/reports/food-manufacturing-2026.pdf)). The 2026 wage review raised award minimums by **4.75% from 1 July** and the National Minimum Wage to A$26.44 an hour ([Fair Work Ombudsman](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/about-us/workplace-laws/annual-wage-review/annual-wage-review-2026); [Squire Patton Boggs](https://www.squirepattonboggs.com/insights/publications/2026-annual-wage-review-decision-fwc-increases-the-national-minimum-wage-and-award-rates/)).

Aged care adds a regulatory push. Quality Standard 6, on food and nutrition, has applied since 1 November 2025 ([Department of Health](https://www.health.gov.au/node/49147); [ACQSC](https://agedcarequality.gov.au/resource-library/standard-6-food-and-dining)). It covers about 204,000 people in residential care ([GEN](https://www.gen-agedcaredata.gov.au/getmedia/7074af35-42f7-4029-b661-cd9c43e52b19/People-using-aged-care-fact-sheet-2025.pdf?ext=.pdf)), who are fed on roughly **A$15.49 per resident per day** ([Canberra Times](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/9005427/aged-care-meals-barely-15-for-daily-nourishment/)), and many aged-care kitchens still make texture-modified food by hand ([Canberra Times](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/9209166/why-food-is-medicine-for-australias-aged-care-residents/)).

The volumes are big enough to keep robots busy.

### Three checks found cooking and delivery robots but no ingredient-assembly service

**Verifier 1** rated the concept **absent at 65% confidence**, putting the chance of a partial match at 30%. Its nearest finds were A&RT, which builds AI-vision ABB FlexPicker and Formflow tray-feeding systems that pick discrete items and are sold as capital projects ([ABB](https://new.abb.com/news/detail/124256/art-celebrating-20-years-of-robotic-automation-excellence-in-australia)); Lyro, which offers AI pick-and-place as a service but for fresh produce; and dnata's trial of a BOTINKIT cooking robot ([dnata](https://www.dnata.com/media-centre/dnata-showcases-advanced-robot-to-serve-exquisite-asian-cuisine-at-wtce/)). **Verifier 2**, working independently, rated it **absent at 80%**. | Capital (estimate) | **A$2–3M** as Chef's partner, covering 6–8 staff for 18 months, spares and certification. **A$6–10M** for your own stack plus a 20–30-robot fleet, much of which can be asset-financed |

### Chef itself is the biggest threat, so the first 90 days should lock in a supplier and two pilots

The international ambition that makes a partnership with Chef plausible also means it could enter Australia directly or decline to partner. A founder should therefore negotiate exclusivity, minimum-volume and customer-ownership terms, keep an integration capability that works with any vendor, and sign multi-year customer contracts early. The likeliest local fast follower is A&RT, now under Foodmach-led ownership. It already combines ABB robots, AI vision and tray feeding, has supplied Simplot, Ingham's, Nestlé and George Weston Foods ([ABB](https://new.abb.com/news/detail/124256/art-celebrating-20-years-of-robotic-automation-excellence-in-australia)), and could build a Chef-style cell for any single customer. Technical risk sits in Australian products such as sticky sauces, rice, mash and purées, and in daily washdown and uptime. That argues for narrow high-volume products first, paid pilots, a pool of spare robots and local engineers bound by service-level agreements. The model's capital needs and the slow purchasing of state health systems and big retailers call for asset finance and a start with private mid-size producers. The best answer to industrial-relations pushback is to present the robots as relief for hard-to-fill shifts, with displaced staff redeployed.

In the first 30 days, a founder should open partnership talks with Chef while pricing a cobot fallback, run a direct IP Australia owner search for the CHEF mark, and interview 10–15 producers to count the hand-assembly stations on each line. By day 60 the goal is signed letters of intent for two paid pilots, one with an aged-care or hospital producer and one with a private ready-meal brand, plus open conversations about asset finance and the National Reconstruction Fund. By day 90 a demonstration cell should be depositing one high-volume product, with deposits per hour, portion accuracy, uptime and labour hours freed all measured, and a three-year contract should be drafted and ready to sign.

## Idea 2: China put 500 hire exoskeletons on one mountain; Australia has none

### Scenic-site hire spread from Mount Tai to 50+ Chinese sites and into Europe

The business rents lightweight powered hip exoskeletons, which reduce the effort of climbing, by the hour or the day at trailheads and attractions. Revenue comes from direct hire, add-ons bundled with attraction tickets and "assist packs" sold to tour operators. Each site runs either as a staffed fitting kiosk or through the host's own staff on a revenue share.

The format took off at China's Mount Tai. Ten units went into service on 29 January 2025 and drew more than 200 users in their first week at ¥60–80 a use, and **by 15 December 2025 the mountain had 500 units, with peak usage around 80%** ([China Daily](https://subsites.chinadaily.com.cn/shandong/2025-12/15/c_1148057.htm); [Sixth Tone](https://www.sixthtone.com/news/1016685)). By China's National Day holiday in October 2026, more than 50 scenic sites offered hire, including 200+ units at Mount Emei, at prices such as 59 yuan for three hours ([Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261005/1e28c0b43b604723906ff035f877bb7e/c.html); [Guangming](https://m.gmw.cn/2026-10/06/content_39032818.htm); [iTiger](https://www.itiger.com/news/1103801174)). Hangzhou added a shared urban service ([eHangzhou](https://www.ehangzhou.gov.cn/2025-10/29/c_295458.htm)).

The model has also crossed into Europe and beyond. Achensee in Austria rents Hypershell units at €25 for the first hour plus €10 for each extra hour, with up to 15 km per battery ([Achensee](https://www.achensee.com/en/book-experiences/detail/experience/exoskelett-hypershell/)), and Exohire in the UK rents exoskeletons for day trips ([Trustpilot](https://uk.trustpilot.com/review/exohire.co.uk)). Budapest exoskeleton hikes are sold to Australians on Wotif ([Wotif](https://www.wotif.com/things-to-do/budapest-exoskeleton-powered-nature-hike.a51257240.activity-details)), Hypershell itself has run an hourly-rental pop-up in Seoul ([Venture Square](https://www.venturesquare.net/1041675/)), and Arc'teryx's MO/GO powered trousers rent for US$80 a day ([Boss Hunting](https://bosshunting.com.au/style/fashion/arcteryx-mo-go)).

It is accelerating because the hardware became light, cheap and consumer-grade. Hypershell's revised X series starts at US$999 ([AAP](https://www.aap.com.au/aapreleases/cision20260520ae63922/)), Decathlon's E-HIKE trial with 272 participants reported about 25% less leg effort ([Decathlon](https://cocreation.decathlon.fr/en/interactions/11418)), and IDC counted about 19,000 consumer-assist exoskeletons sold in 2025 ([Caixin](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-02-27/in-depth-exoskeletons-offer-consumer-robotics-a-chance-to-find-its-footing-102417653.html)). The negative signals deserve weight too. Hikers at Mount Tai gave mixed reviews ([Advnture](https://www.advnture.com/news/hikers-have-mixed-reviews-after-taking-on-one-of-chinas-toughest-treks-wearing-a-new-ai-powered-exoskeleton)), and a UK review found the devices weaker on rough rock and descents ([Countryfile](https://www.countryfile.com/reviews/hypershell-x-ultra-exoskeleton)). A willingness-to-pay study valued the average uphill benefit at only about US$3.40 an hour once device weight was counted ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/putting-a-price-on-exoskeleton-assistance-puts-users-in-the-drivers-seat-of-honing-the-tech-209491)). Mount Tai's 80% is also a peak-season figure, not an annual average.

### Local stock, record park visits and Chinese tourists make the timing right

Since **20 May 2026, Hypershell has sold its X series in Australia** ([AAP](https://www.aap.com.au/aapreleases/cision20260520ae63922/)), so a hire fleet no longer needs importing. Demand comes from three groups. NSW national parks drew 65.5M visits in FY2024–25, 7.8M of them to the Blue Mountains, and 72% of overseas tourists to NSW visited a park ([Canberra Times](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/9229654/nsw-national-parks-record-65-million-visits-why-demand-surged/); [New Daily](https://www.thenewdaily.com.au/travel/destinations/2026/07/10/nsw-most-visited-national-parks)). Chinese visitors numbered **1,040,140 in 2025 (+17%) and spent A$11.2bn (+39%)** ([TRA](https://www.tra.gov.au/content/dam/austrade-assets/global/wip/tra/documents/market-profiles/tra-market-profiles-china-2025.pdf)). Their arrivals passed 1.1M in the year to June 2026 ([China Daily HK](https://www.chinadailyhk.com/article/638525)), China supplied 23.3% of NSW arrivals in February 2026 ([Destination NSW](https://www.destinationnsw.com.au/newsroom/lunar-new-year-drives-chinese-visitor-surge)), and these travellers come from the one market where exoskeleton hire is already mainstream. Older travellers make up the third group: Intrepid's bookings from travellers aged 55+ rose 52%, and about 4.5M Australians are 65 or older ([The Senior](https://www.thesenior.com.au/story/9327678/more-and-more-older-australians-are-signing-up-for-adventure-travel/)).

Natural first sites include Scenic World, with 1M+ visitors a year ([Blue Mountains Gazette](https://www.bluemountainsgazette.com.au/story/4692627/hitting-million-milestone/)), and Cradle Mountain, with 300k+ ([Grey Nomads](https://www.thegreynomads.com.au/?p=63622)). The unmet need shows in tour conditions. A Blue Mountains walk requires "excellent mobility and no history of knee injuries" ([Exploreo](https://exploreo.com/tours/t/218530/walk-the-blue-mountains)), a Cradle Mountain tour "does not cater for people with access needs" ([Discover Tasmania](https://discovertasmania.com.au/things-to-do/tours/mcdermottstreasureislandcoaches/cradle-mountain-national-park-day-tour/)), and Intrepid markets a portered Maria Island walk to people limited "by injury or age" ([Intrepid](https://www.intrepidtravel.com/au/australia/tasmanias-maria-island-walk-portered-174875/tripnotes)).

### Two verifiers and a final check found only industrial hire and free brand demos

**Verifier 1** rated exoskeleton hire **absent at 75%** after 26 presence searches. Its closest finds were Hypershell's Australian store and its "Host Program", in which owners give free try-outs ([Hypershell AU](https://au.hypershell.tech/blogs/news/want-to-try-hypershell-x-meet-a-host-near-you)); SpanSet's demo, trial and hire of passive industrial exosuits made by Auxivo ([SpanSet](https://www.spanset.com/au-en/section/news/mining-safety-innovation-the-role-of-wearable-exosuits)); clinical rehabilitation exoskeletons such as Wandercraft's Atalante X, a Class IIa medical device ([Wandercraft](https://www.wandercraft.eu/articles/wandercraft-announces-availability-in-australia)); and the offshore Budapest hike sold on Wotif. **Verifier 2**, searching independently in English and Chinese and covering New Zealand as well, rated it **absent at 80%**. It found that the Host Program is "completely free" and lists no Australian hosts ([Hypershell](https://hypershell.tech/pages/try-hypershell-exoskeletons)), that SpanSet rents exosuits on a rent-to-buy basis with a two-week minimum at A$7,022.61 per suit ([SpanSet AU](https://www.spanset.com/au-en/category/exoskeleton-7142)), and that the nearest leisure equivalent is the free loan of all-terrain TrailRider wheelchairs at Tidbinbilla ([ACT Parks](https://www.parks.act.gov.au/things-to-do/trailrider-all-terrain-wheelchair)). **The final agent** confirmed absence. It noted that Hypershell's new Halo leg exoskeleton (US$2,299, shipping from November 2026) comes with retail demo zones expanding across Europe and none announced for Australia ([IFA](https://www.ifa-berlin.com/programme/hypershell_press_conference)), and that Australian walking-tour operators make no mention of exoskeletons.

The safe claim is **"the first exoskeleton hire for hikers and tourists in Australia."** "First exoskeleton hire" is not safe, because SpanSet already hires out industrial exosuits, and neither is "first chance to try one", because Hypershell hosts offer free try-outs.

### Injury, weather and the brand itself are the risks; test over summer within 90 days

Injury is the biggest risk: falls on descents, overexertion or poor fit among older and less fit users. The mitigations are staff fitting and briefing, a health and suitability questionnaire, route restrictions, models with descent assist, public liability and product cover, and a log of every incident. Utilisation, seasonality and weather come next, and are best handled with host revenue shares, fleets that rotate between sites, bundles with tour operators and a focus on Chinese holiday peaks. Durability is the third risk, because there is no fleet programme and batteries wear out, so hold 10–15% spare units and batteries, build local repair skills and source from two brands. The likeliest fast followers are Hypershell itself, which now sells locally, and attractions that buy their own fleets. The only moat is know-how in fitting, routes and insurance, plus exclusive host contracts, and it is thin, so speed matters more here than in any other idea. Positioning must stay recreational, because marketing to people with mobility conditions could turn the hire into the supply of a medical device.

In the first 30 days, negotiate terms for 20–40 units with Hypershell and Dnsys and pitch Scenic World a revenue-share concession. By day 60, bind insurance and a waiver framework, apply for a Parks Eco Pass if any use will be inside national parks, and sign two Sydney inbound tour operators for a Blue Mountains "assist pack". By day 90, run a staffed pilot through the December–January summer peak, ahead of Lunar New Year in February 2027, and track hires per day, average ticket, incidents and downtime before committing to more sites.

## Idea 3: Robotic massage can undercut human therapists if the hardware stays cheap

### Aescape's rise and insolvency show both the demand and the trap

A robotic massage bay pairs a padded bed with one or two robot arms. The customer books by app, the system scans the body, and the robot delivers a programmed massage with no therapist present. The operator earns per session or through memberships, either by placing bays in host venues on a revenue share or by running its own studio.

The US leader, Aescape, showed both the demand and the danger. On the way up it raised **US$83M in March 2025** (US$128M in total) and announced expansion to 60 Equinox locations ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250304931224/en/Aescape-Secures-$83MM-in-Strategic-Funding-Announces-U.S.-Expansion-with-Equinox)), added a Massage Envy franchise roll-out ([Athletech News](https://athletechnews.com/massage-envy-adds-aescape-robotic-massages/)), and charged US$45 for 30 minutes and US$80 for 60 ([Audacy](https://www.audacy.com/kdawn/news/robot-masseuse-debuts-in-san-antonio)), or from US$29 in Chicago ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/local/chicago/2025/09/16/robotic-massage-chicago-aescape-equinox)). Then it fell. Its operating assets were sold in a January 2026 foreclosure to a successor, Aescape Recovery Inc., and in April 2026 the original company made a general assignment for the benefit of creditors after defaulting on a secured loan first held by Silicon Valley Bank ([Athletech News](https://athletechnews.com/ai-massage-company-aescape-insolvency-proceedings/)). Fitt Insider reported the insolvency on 8 May 2026 ([Fitt Insider](https://insider.fitt.co/robot-massage-company-aescape-enters-insolvency/)), against a **reported shortfall of US$157M** ([FitTech Global](https://www.fittechglobal.com/fit-tech-news/Robot-massage-company-Aescape-Inc-goes-bust-with-shortfall-of-157-million/362902)). The reported causes were software, supply and delivery bottlenecks, weak demand and, according to new CEO Frank Britt, the "wrong go-to-market model" ([Athletech News](https://athletechnews.com/new-aescape-ceo-frank-britt-says-company-will-continue-under-revamped-business-model/)). The successor moved from pure robots-as-a-service to selling hardware with a platform fee ([The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/5-lessons-robotics-entrepreneurs-from-aescapes-business-model-pivot/)). Its price list now starts at US$125k per unit (about US$3k a month financed) plus US$10k a year in platform fees, and its own example shows US$7,280 a month in gross revenue and a 20-month payback ([Aescape](https://aescape.com/pricing)).

The lesson is mostly about capital and model rather than demand: Aescape placed expensive, debt-financed hardware on a pure rental model. But "weak demand" was also among the reported causes, which is why the Australian plan below avoids buying premium hardware and avoids paying fixed rent.

Growth has shifted to China. Yiqi Future, spun out of NetEase, closed a Series A on 29 May 2026 ([36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/en/newsflashes/3830281161860998)) and took about **RMB 40M of orders in Q1 2026** ([RC Caijing](https://m.rccaijing.com/news-7466023528598009185.html)). Chinese media describe the sector as rising ([CLS](https://www.cls.cn/detail/2470109)) but also report complaints about quality and heavy discounting in Beijing ([Huxiu](https://www.huxiu.com/article/4885592.html?f=rss); [PEdaily](https://news.pedaily.cn/202608/568057.shtml); [Jiemian](https://www.jiemian.com/article/12569240.html)). France's Capsix leases its iYU robot for about €2,700 a month ([Spa Business](https://www.spabusiness.com/spa-business-magazine/Industry-insight-AI-and-robot-massage/37939)) and has delivered 30k+ massages ([Hospitality ON](https://hospitality-on.com/fr/marketing/lhotellerie-lheure-des-robots-immersion-au-ki-space-hotel)), and since March 2026 Earthlite has been its exclusive distributor for the Americas ([Leisure Opportunities](https://www.leisureopportunities.co.uk/news/Earthlite-and-Capsix-partner-to-develop-AI-powered-robot-massage-solutions/362566)). Robosculptor launched in the US in July 2025 ([Spa Business](https://www.spabusiness.com/wellness-products-and-services/Robosculptor-to-host-first-US-AI-massage-pop-up-at-Four-Seasons-Hotel-Las-Vegas/356518)). The model is accelerating because six-axis cobots and 3D body scanning have become cheap and Chinese makers have fresh capital. A vendor forecast of growth from US$156m in 2026 to US$2.15bn in 2036 should be read as marketing ([FMI](https://www.futuremarketinsights.com/reports/robotic-massage-and-wellness-market)).

### Australian therapists are scarce and expensive, and recovery clubs are multiplying

A 60-minute remedial massage costs A$105–175 in Sydney's eastern suburbs ([Fresha](https://www.fresha.com/lp/nl/tt/genezende-massages/au-sydney/randwick)), and 30 minutes can cost A$85 ([Fresha](https://www.fresha.com/lp/pt/tt/massagens-corretivas/au-sydney/milsons-point)). The workforce is thin. Victoria has about 4,473 massage therapists, growing about 1% a year ([Vic Gov](https://www.vic.gov.au/massage-therapist)), and a workforce survey found that **73.8% of therapists work 20 hours a week or less** ([UTS](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/37259)). The spa market is worth about A$2.8bn ([IBISWorld](https://www.ibisworld.com/au/market-size/health-wellness-spas/)).

Ready-made host sites are multiplying. P3 Recovery ([Time Out](https://www.timeout.com/melbourne/health-and-beauty/p3-recovery)), the O-Studio franchise ([SEEK Business](https://www.seekbusiness.com.au/franchise/o-studio/83482)), XtraClubs with app-key entry in Marrickville and Manly ([Men's Fitness](https://mensfitnessonline.com.au/xtralubs-marrickville-and-manly/)) and Fitness First's Revive concept ([Fitt Insider](https://insider.fitt.co/fitness-first-australia-debuts-revive-recovery-concept/)) all sell recovery to people who already pay for it. Chinese robot makers are also building Australian channels, as AGIBOT's July 2026 partner conference for Australia and New Zealand in Melbourne showed ([CNFin](https://www.cnfin.com/gs-lb/detail/20260717/4442370_1.html)).

### Every check found chairs, prototypes or human therapists, never a robot service

**Verifier 1** rated robotic massage **absent at 75%**. The nearest things it found were Swinburne University's 2019 laser-guided cobot research prototype ([Swinburne](https://swinburne.edu.au/news/2019/06/swinburne-researchers-are-training-robots-to-relieve-chronic-pain)), massage chairs, Ceragem thermal roller beds and Blys's on-demand human therapists. **Verifier 2**, which also searched Chinese-language channels, rated it **absent at 85%** and found that Mornington's "AI-enhanced massage" is delivered by human therapists ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2025/09/19/3153204/0/en/Mornington-Clinics-Transform-Pain-Relief-with-AI-Enhanced-Massage-Mornington.html)). **The final agent** confirmed absence. It found no Australian operator, distributor, pilot, trade mark or job ad, only chairs and equipment retailers in social-media results, and no Aescape filing in IP Australia. Its nearest regional precedent was "Emma", a robot masseuse in a Singapore clinic in 2017 ([Malay Mail](https://www.malaymail.com/news/tech/gadgets/2017/10/09/fancy-a-neck-rub-a-robot-can-do-the-job-in-singapore/1483287)). The residual risk is a Chinese-Australian massage or traditional Chinese medicine shop that has quietly imported a robot bed and advertises it only on WeChat or Xiaohongshu.

The safe claim is **"Australia's first commercial robotic massage service."** "First AI massage" is not safe, because Mornington already uses that label, and neither is "first massage robot", because Swinburne's research came first.

### Launch brief: Chinese cobots break even at 2.5 sessions a day, premium robots need 7.4 (estimates)

The figures below are estimates from the wellness launch brief and assume US$1 ≈ A$1.54.

### Supplier failure is the headline risk; place the first bays within 90 days

Supplier and after-sales failure is the headline risk: Aescape has already been through insolvency, and Capsix and Yiqi have no Australian presence. Leasing or paying per session rather than buying, contracting for local spares, remote support and software escrow, and sourcing from both a premium and a Chinese supplier all limit the damage. Low utilisation and fading novelty call for host revenue shares instead of fixed rent, memberships, corporate subscriptions and 15-minute express sessions. Fast followers could be recovery chains importing Chinese robots directly, Chinese makers building their own channels, or Capsix or Aescape Recovery appointing a distributor. Exclusive Australian distribution with one maker, and exclusivity with each host, is the best defence.

By day 60, sign two to four recovery-club or gym hosts, complete the safety case and insurance, and assemble booking and access from off-the-shelf tools (about A$30–80k, estimate). By day 90, open the first bays and track sessions per day against the 2.5 and 7.4 break-evens.

## Idea 4: Robotic manicures need a hardware partner more than a market

### 10Beauty absorbed Clockwork and raised US$23.5M for in-store placements

A robotic manicure station scans the nails and paints them in 10–45 minutes with no technician. The operator earns per service, from memberships and from polish pods, and stations usually sit inside a retailer or salon that takes a revenue share and supplies light supervision.

10Beauty leads the field. It raised **US$23.5M** led by Story Ventures in 2026, taking its total to about US$70M, and has about **850 machines agreed** ([Cosmetics Business](https://cosmeticsbusiness.com/10beauty-raises-further-23-5-million-funding); [Retail Tech Innovation Hub](https://retailtechinnovationhub.com/home/2026/7/2/10beauty-bags-235-million-in-funding-to-expand-robotic-manicures-across-retailers-and-salons)). Its machines run in Ulta and Nordstrom stores and at a Chicago salon partner ([American Salon](https://www.americansalon.com/nails/robot-manicures-are-coming-more-us-shops-salons); [Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/10beauty-raises-23-5-million-to-expand-robotic-manicures-to-retail-and-salons/)), charging about US$30 for 25–45 minutes ([Personal Care Insights](https://www.personalcareinsights.com/news/robotic-manicure-startup-funding.html)). In February 2025 it bought its rival Clockwork, which had painted 500k+ nails before closing its consumer machines ([SF Standard](https://sfstandard.com/2025/02/06/sf-robot-manicure-company-clockwork-closes/); [BeautyMatter](https://beautymatter.com/articles/10beauty-acquires-autonomous-manicure-competitor-clockwork)). Clockwork had run in US airports ([Miami International Airport](https://news.miami-airport.com/xpresspa-debuts-clockwork-robotic-manicures-at-mia/)) and in a US$10 Target pilot that still needed an attendant ([KTVZ/CNN](https://ktvz.com/lifestyle/technology/cnn-social-media-technology/2022/05/05/heres-what-its-like-to-have-a-robot-paint-your-nails-for-10/); [RetailWire](https://old.retailwire.com/?p=151046)).

The other players are smaller. Umia sells machines and takes a commission through its marketplace; it is installed at Disney Shanghai and has been trialled in Ireland ([Scratch](https://www.scratchmagazine.co.uk/news/ai-nail-art-machine-disney-shanghai); [Caplight](https://www.caplight.com/company/umia); [Offaly Express](https://www.offalyexpress.ie/national/i-tried-the-worlds-first-ai-manicure-and-this-is-how-it-went-6716317)). Nimble, a home robot, raised US$1.8M from 5,877 Kickstarter backers and was a CES 2024 honoree ([Kicktraq](https://www.kicktraq.com/projects/nimble/nimble-salon-quality-nails-from-the-comfort-of-your-home/); [CES](https://www.ces.tech/ces-innovation-awards/2024/nimble-beauty-the-ai-powered-manicure-device-for-home-use/)). Luum, a robot that applies eyelash extensions, signals the likely terms for beauty robots: about US$125k per unit plus 30% of service fees ([Beauty Independent](https://www.beautyindependent.com/ulta-beauty-retail-bound-robotic-eyelash-extension-service-luum-precision-lash/)). The category is accelerating because retailers want services that bring people into stores, and placing machines inside retailers solves the attendant problem that sank standalone kiosks. The negatives are real: Clockwork's consumer business closed, services take 25–45 minutes, and 10Beauty is committed to the US.

### Expensive nail labour and light rules for polish-only service leave room

Australia's waxing and nail-salon industry is worth about **A$1.9bn** ([IBISWorld](https://www.ibisworld.com/australia/industry/personal-waxing-and-nail-salons-in-australia/5126/)). Labour is costly: beauty therapists earn a median of about A$1,200 a week ([Vic Gov](https://vic.gov.au/beauty-therapist)), and nail technicians are advertised at A$65–85k ([SEEK](https://au.seek.com/nail-jobs)). Prices are low, though. An express manicure starts at A$15 in the suburbs ([Fresha](https://fresha.com/lp/en/tt/manicures/in/au-sydney/matraville)) and costs A$30–46 at CBD day spas ([WhatClinic](https://www.whatclinic.com/beauty-salons/australasia/manicure?page=1)), so a robot must win on convenience and placement, not price. Beauty retail in Australia is moving toward in-store services, as Mecca's Melbourne flagship ([PowerCommerce](https://powercommerce.com/de/blogs/ecommerce-hub/mecca-s-new-melbourne-flagship-a-bold-step-in-experiential-retail)) and Sephora's flagships show ([Shopping Centre News](https://shoppingcentrenews.com.au/tag/flagship/)). Regulation is light if the service stays polish-only. NSW skin-penetration rules cover cuticle cutting and acrylics, not polish ([Canterbury-Bankstown Council](https://www.cbcity.nsw.gov.au/index.php/environment/public-health/skin-penetration)), Brisbane requires no licence for nail services ([Brisbane City Council](https://www.brisbane.qld.gov.au/laws-and-permits/licences-permits-regulations/personal-appearance-services-licence)), and Victoria requires annual council registration of the premises as medium-risk ([Cardinia Shire](https://www.cardinia.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-07/Guide%20to%20setting%20up%20a%20health%20business%20registration%20%281%29.pdf)).

### No robot paints nails in Australia, but 10Beauty owns the trade mark

**Verifier 1** rated robotic manicures **absent at 78%** after 40 searches. Its only Australia-linked finds were Arbor Australia's 2014 nail-art printing kiosk prototype ([Microsoft Australia](https://news.microsoft.com/en-au/2014/04/29/the-internet-of-your-things-the-internet-of-things-nailed-arbor-australia/)) and the **10BEAUTY trade mark, No. 2551881** ([IP Australia](https://search.ipaustralia.gov.au/trademarks/search/view/2551881/details)). **Verifier 2** rated it **absent at 75%**. It found that the mark is an international registration claiming US priority from 9 May 2024, entered on the Australian register on 18 August 2025, and covering the manicure device (Class 8) and polish pods (Class 3). It also found that an apparent Umia listing at Sydney Showground was an artefact of the Sydney Boat Show ([Superyachts](https://www.superyachts.com/news/story/sydney-boat-show-2026-australias-premier-boating-event-returns-bigger-and-better-18115/)). **A third, targeted check** found no polish-painting robot sold or shipped to Australians. Nimble "currently ships to the United States of America only" ([Nimble Help](https://help.nimblebeauty.com/en-US/articles/orders-and-returns-39903)), although its 2021 Kickstarter drew backers from 25+ countries ([Jotup](https://jotup.co/node/1306364)); Aurami is a pre-production prototype ([Gizmocrowd](https://www.gizmocrowd.com/post/aurami-nail-machine-review)); and the "Automatic Nail Painting Machine" listings on Amazon Australia are nail-art printers ([Amazon AU](https://www.amazon.com.au/Printer-Automatic-Painting-Printing-Resolution/dp/B0FND26J98)). **The final agent** confirmed absence with two caveats: the trade mark, and the possibility that a few Nimble units arrived through Kickstarter or freight forwarders.

The safe claim is **"the first in-store robotic polish-painting (robotic manicure) service in Australia."** "First automated nail kiosk" is not safe, because Arbor's 2014 prototype came first, and neither is "nothing similar exists", because nail-art printers are on sale. A founder should also avoid any brand name that conflicts with 10BEAUTY, and should treat 10Beauty as a likely future entrant, partner or licensor rather than an absent competitor.

### Launch brief: A$29–35 polish-only, viable only inside a host store (estimates)

### Supplier access decides this idea; run parallel talks and a printer pilot in 90 days

The lack of a hardware partner is the deciding risk, because 10Beauty is focused on the US and Umia is small. The answer is to talk to Umia, 10Beauty and a Chinese manufacturer in parallel, to offer a retailer-anchored launch with minimum-volume commitments in return for exclusivity, and to test demand with nail-art printers first. Throughput is the second risk, since services take 25–45 minutes; in-store placements staffed by the host, a polish-only format of 15–25 minutes and several machines per site address it. Quality, hygiene and regulation call for single-use pods, an in-app check that nails are bare and healthy, no cuticle work, advance council clearance and AICIS registration. The most likely fast follower is 10Beauty itself arriving with a large retailer, which is why becoming its Australian licensee may be the better outcome. Printer sellers relabelling their machines as "robot manicures" are a nuisance rather than a threat.

In the first 30 days, open talks with Umia and 10Beauty, approach two beauty retailers as anchors, clear the brand name against the 10BEAUTY mark, and confirm how NSW and Queensland councils treat a polish-only service. By day 60, start AICIS registration and run a two-week nail-art-printer pop-up priced at A$29–35 to test demand. By day 90, sign a supplier term sheet with Australian exclusivity tied to a retailer launch.

## Idea 5: Self-service IPL studios hinge on one insurer saying yes

### ONESELF built 86 unstaffed studios in Japan, but growth has stalled

A self-service IPL studio rents out private rooms fitted with professional intense pulsed light (IPL) machines, which customers use on themselves to remove hair. Rooms are booked and unlocked by app and paid for per timed session or by membership, and after a first-visit consultation no therapist is in the room. The operator earns session and membership revenue without paying for clinic labour.

ONESELF opened its first store in Numazu in 2021. By 31 January 2026 it had **86 stores and 76,000 members**, and its 90th store opened in Maebashi on 26 September 2026 ([san-tatsu](https://san-tatsu.jp/pressrelease/556225/)); its 82nd had opened on 7 June 2025 ([Web Tan](https://webtan.impress.co.jp/r/prtimes/items/000000083.000104823)). In 2022 its regular prices were ¥3,500, ¥5,500 and ¥7,500 for 20, 40 and 60 minutes, roughly A$35, A$55 and A$75 ([BCN Retail](https://www.bcnretail.com/news/detail/20221107_303451.html)), and its machines include a gentler SHR mode ([san-tatsu](https://san-tatsu.jp/pressrelease/486537/)). Rival franchises such as King's Salon also operate ([DreamNews](https://www.dreamnews.jp/press/0000210523)), as part of Japan's wider economy of unstaffed self-service formats ([Kabutan](https://finance.matsui.co.jp/news/610390/index)).

The negative signal is clear. **ONESELF opened about 80 stores in its first four years, then only about 8 in the 15 months from June 2025 to September 2026.** An ONESELF franchise executive describes a "chain of bankruptcies" among Japanese hair-removal salons and admits difficulty attracting women customers ([Livedoor](https://news.livedoor.com/article/detail/29391943/)). This is the weakest growth signal of the five: a proven format whose home market is saturating, not a breakout.

### Australian clinic prices are high, and only Tasmania licenses IPL

Australia's hair-removal market was worth about US$950.5M in 2024 and is forecast to reach about US$1.46bn by 2033 ([IMARC](https://www.imarcgroup.com/australia-hair-removal-market-bolstered-rising)). Home IPL devices already sell about US$120M a year, which shows Australians accept operating IPL themselves ([Nexdigm](https://www.nexdigm.com/market-research/report-store/australia-ipl-hair-removal-devices-market-research-report/)). Clinic list prices leave a wide gap: a full leg costs A$650 at a Melbourne chain ([WhatClinic](https://www.whatclinic.com/beauty-salons/australia/victoria/melbourne/blossomed-ipl-exhibition-street)) and A$545 for women in Perth ([Skin Resus](https://skinresus.com.au/prices/ipl-laser-hair-removal-prices/)), whereas a self-session at A$75–99 could cover the same area.

Regulation is permissive. ARPANSA says no licence is needed for light-based cosmetic treatments except in Tasmania, and except for lasers in Queensland and Western Australia ([ARPANSA](https://www.arpansa.gov.au/advice-consumers-lasers-ipl-devices-and-led-phototherapy-cosmetic-treatments-and-beauty-therapy); [ARPANSA](https://arpansa.gov.au/understanding-radiation/sources-radiation/more-radiation-sources/lasers-and-intense-pulsed-light-ipl)). An IPL-only studio is therefore workable everywhere except Tasmania, while a studio using diode lasers would not be workable in Queensland, Western Australia or Tasmania. The unstaffed format itself is already proven locally. Unstaffed tanning studios such as La Dorada use app booking and Bluetooth door access ([Shepparton News](https://www.sheppnews.com.au/?p=258544)), and Reverie in Canberra opened in August 2025 as a 24/7 self-guided wellness space with an automated spray-tan booth ([Time Out](https://www.timeout.com/australia/news/australias-first-24/7-self-guided-wellness-space-has-opened-but-not-where-youd-expect-100625)). Professional IPL devices are included on the ARTG through Australian suppliers ([TGA 327736](https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/artg/327736); [TGA 341737](https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/artg/341737)).

### No Australian studio lets customers operate IPL; one New Zealand community club does

**Verifier 1** rated the concept **absent at 80%** after 21 searches in English, Japanese, Korean and Chinese across seven cities. It found only home devices, staffed clinics and tanning studios, and produced the state-by-state workability analysis summarised above. **Verifier 2** rated it **absent for Australia at 75%**, but found one material caveat across the Tasman. The Sea and Stone Spa (free IPL) Club, run by Gender Minorities Aotearoa with RainbowYOUTH, is a private, non-commercial club in which transgender members treat their own facial hair after a staff demonstration, for a one-off NZ$20 fee ([RainbowYOUTH](https://ry.org.nz/iplclub); [Gender Minorities Aotearoa](https://genderminorities.com/services/ipl-club)). **The final agent** confirmed absence. It found that Aesthetic Bureau rents IPL systems only to clinics ([Experiment](https://experiment.com/users/abureau)), and that the only consumer IPL rental listing anywhere was on a UK peer-to-peer site ([hygglo](https://hygglo.com/uk/i/hire-philips-lumea-ipl--prevents-hair-regrowth-02550006)).

The safe claim is **"the first commercial self-service IPL studio in Australia."** A founder should not claim to be "the first place in Australia or New Zealand where customers operate IPL themselves", because of the New Zealand club.

### Launch brief: A$45–99 sessions, break-even near 18 a day across four rooms (estimates)

The figures below are estimates from the wellness launch brief.

| Element | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Devices | An ARTG-included professional IPL from an Australian supplier, not a diode laser. It should have presets locked by Fitzpatrick skin type, a melanin sensor that refuses to fire above the preset limit, and skin types V–VI excluded at launch, plus session-locked activation, door interlocks, goggles, contact cooling and usage logs |
| Price (estimate) | A$45 for 20 minutes, A$75 for 40 and A$99 for 60. Memberships at A$99 a month for two 40-minute sessions, or A$149 for unlimited off-peak use. An A$29 first consultation and patch test |
| Unit economics (estimate) | A four-room studio open 15 hours a day, at A$55 realised per session, has fixed costs of A$27.6k a month. At 20% utilisation (18 sessions a day) it makes about A$485 a month, and at 35% (31.5 sessions a day) about A$21,549. Break-even is about 17.7 sessions a day |
| Beachhead sites | Brisbane, the Gold Coast or Sydney first, then Melbourne once council registration is scoped, and never Tasmania. Upper-floor, fringe-CBD or suburban strip sites near gyms, universities and transport, or rooms inside 24/7 gyms and recovery clubs |
| Approvals and compliance | **Insurer sign-off on the operating protocol before signing any lease.** Cosmetic claims only ("hair reduction"). Victorian registration of beauty premises, and confirmation from NSW and Queensland councils of how IPL is classed. EESS and RCM marking, privacy for health information, Australian Consumer Law, and AS/NZS 4173 as the benchmark for safety management |
| Capital (estimate) | **A$0.96–1.82M** for 3 sites with 4 rooms each, or **A$1.5–2.9M** for 5 sites |

### Burns are the existential risk, so the first 90 days are an insurance gate, not a fit-out

ARPANSA's "Does beauty burn?" study counted 95 media-reported injuries (60 from lasers, 29 from IPL); about 20% were permanent, and **93% of cases with a stated cause involved operator factors** ([ARPANSA](https://www.arpansa.gov.au/does-beauty-burn-study-media-reports-burns-cosmetic-nir-devices-australia); [ARPANSA](https://arpansa.gov.au/media-reports-show-energy-based-cosmetic-treatment-harms-gaps-regulation)). In this model the customer is the operator, and one Australian IPL burn claim settled for A$76,000 ([BizCover](https://www.bizcover.com.au/?p=15419)). The mitigations are an in-person first visit covering skin typing, a medication screen and a patch test with a 24–48-hour wait; a remote video supervisor who can pause any device; an intercom in every room; no minors; and conservative energy settings. Insurance is the gate. The UK's BABTAC endorsement makes cover conditional on qualified operators ([BABTAC](https://WWW.BABTAC.COM/userfiles/files/BABTAC%20Laser%20and%20Light%20Therapies%20including%20IPL%20up%20to%20skin%20type%20VI%20extension%20Endorsement.pdf)), and no Australian insurer was found that covers customer-operated IPL, so insurability should be treated as a go/no-go decision, pursued through a specialist broker, with a fallback in which a therapist assists with express sessions. Regulation could also tighten. In 2019 the TGA proposed regulating IPL hair-removal equipment whatever its stated purpose ([TGA](https://www.tga.gov.au/sites/default/files/consultation-changes-number-definitions-and-scope-medical-device-regulatory-framework-australia.pdf)), ARPANSA flagged regulatory gaps in October 2025, and Health Victoria warns about untrained operators ([Health Victoria](https://health.vic.gov.au/radiation/lasers-ipl-and-led-devices-for-cosmetic-treatments-and-beauty-therapy)), so the studio should be designed to exceed a likely future standard and its leases kept short. As for fast followers, laser chains could add self-service rooms, and unstaffed tanning operators already own the access technology. The moat is the safety protocol and the insurance arrangement, not the room.

In the first 30 days, take the protocol to specialist brokers and underwriters, get legal advice on TGA, state and consumer law, select an ARTG-included IPL device that can lock its presets, and sweep Google Maps, Instagram and Xiaohongshu for any hidden competitor. By day 60, run a supervised pilot with a qualified therapist on site to build safety data, and invite ARPANSA or state health comment on the framework. By day 90, sign a short, flexible lease for the first studio, but only once an insurance term sheet is in hand. If no insurer will write cover, stop, or pivot to therapist-assisted express sessions.

## One automated-wellness platform should carry ideas 3 to 5

The wellness launch brief concludes that massage, manicure and IPL share a real platform but not a single shop. The shared layer is one app and membership wallet, smart-lock access, and a remote supervision hub in which one qualified therapist on video can cover first IPL consultations, device pauses and massage exceptions across all sites. Cleaning contracts, one insurance programme and one brand complete it. The brief estimates this saves **15–25% of overheads** compared with three separate businesses. That saving is an estimate, because no Australian operator yet combines the three. The brief also proposes cross-selling through a single "wellness credits" membership at around A$149 a month, spendable on a 30-minute massage, a 40-minute IPL session or an express manicure (estimate).

At site level the fit is weak, because manicure needs high-footfall retail while IPL and massage suit private rooms in cheaper space. Australian retail rents also make single-unit mall kiosks uneconomic: Westfield Sydney pop-ups have cost A$800–1,000 a week ([Ragtrader](https://www.ragtrader.com.au/archive/exclusive-insight-into-westfield-development)), and a Westfield Carindale kiosk lease runs at A$2,557 a week ([BusinessSales](https://app.businesssales.com.au/?p=507537)). The answer is a hub-and-spokes model, with private-room studios that combine IPL and massage, hosted massage bays in gyms and recovery clubs, and retail manicure spokes. Unstaffed wellness already works locally. Reverie in Canberra combines app booking, Bluetooth access and an automated spray-tan booth under one brand ([Time Out](https://www.timeout.com/australia/news/australias-first-24/7-self-guided-wellness-space-has-opened-but-not-where-youd-expect-100625)), and XtraClubs uses an app as a digital key ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/bb/app/xtraclubs/id6479224485)).

The recommended sequence follows regulatory weight and supplier access. **Massage comes first**, placed with recovery and gym hosts, because it is the most lightly regulated. **IPL comes second**, after insurer sign-off and a supervised pilot, co-located with massage in private-room studios. **Manicure comes last**, added as a retail channel once a hardware partner signs. The IPL risk must be ring-fenced in a separate legal entity with its own insurance, and possibly a sub-brand, until it has a safety record, so that one burn story cannot sink the massage business. The brief estimates capital for three flagship studios, each with three IPL rooms, two cobot massage bays and one or two manicure machines, at **A$1.4–2.9M**. The other risks of combining the three are three foreign suppliers with different service regimes, insurers pricing the whole portfolio on IPL risk, Victorian registration that brings the whole premises under council inspection, and misuse of unstaffed rooms, which identity-verified accounts, session-locked devices and CCTV limited to common areas can contain.

## Three reserves passed absence checks but fail on risk or evidence

**Automated, attendant-assisted gold-buying kiosks** were the strongest reserve. They weigh, test and pay for unwanted jewellery, and two independent verifiers each rated them **absent at 80%**. The overseas demand is real: Kinghood's "Smart Gold Store" terminals reached about 40 Chinese cities by September 2024 ([Kitco](https://www.kitco.com/news/article/2024-09-25/international-gold-atm-offers-self-service-buying-and-selling-precious)), recovery volumes rose 589% year on year in H1 2025 ([36Kr](https://www.36kr.com/p/3407741585952392)), and Shanghai's mall machines drew queues as record prices pushed people to sell in early 2026 ([Malay Mail/AFP](https://malaymail.com/news/money/2026/02/02/chinas-gold-rush-goes-into-reverse-as-record-prices-send-sellers-to-mall-recycling-machines/207570)). Two problems keep the idea out of the top five. First, Kinghood, the reference supplier, is distressed, with products linked to it defaulting on about RMB 7–8bn ([CTEE](https://www.ctee.com.tw/news/20251224701345-430803)). Second, Australian second-hand-dealer law forces a human into the process. NSW imposes a 14-day holding period, records to police within 3 days and a possible 56-day police hold ([NSW Government](https://www.nsw.gov.au/business-and-economy/running-a-business/industry-specific-business-requirements/pawnbrokers-and-second-hand-dealers)), while Victoria and Queensland impose 7-day holds ([Consumer Affairs Victoria](https://www.consumer.vic.gov.au/licensing-and-registration/second-hand-dealers-and-pawnbrokers/running-your-business/keeping-records); [Queensland Government](https://www.qld.gov.au/community/fair-trading/regulated-industries-licensing-and-legislation/second-hand-dealing-and-pawnbroking-industry-regulation/legal-requirements)). Kiosks must also defend against tungsten fakes, whose density is close to gold's ([UMK journal](https://journal.umk.edu.my/index.php/teniat/article/download/745/524/4665)). The economics could still work. CHOICE once found cash-for-gold offers of 39–89% of metal value ([CHOICE](https://www.choice.com.au/shopping/everyday-shopping/shopping-centres/articles/cash-for-gold)), so paying 92–96% of spot would compete (estimate), and AUSTRAC's precious-metal dealer rules cover only cash or virtual-asset deals of A$10k or more, so bank-transfer payouts stay outside them ([AUSTRAC](https://www.austrac.gov.au/new-austrac/designated-services-newly-regulated-entities/precious-metals-stones-and-products-designated-services)). The brief estimates A$1.5–3M of equity plus a gold float of A$0.5–3.5M.

**AI hair-washing salons** were checked only once (**absent at 70%**). Shenzhen had 500+ AI hair-wash stores in 2025 ([EyeShenzhen](https://www.eyeshenzhen.com/content/2025-05/22/content_31575578.htm)), Faxiaoka planned a Hong Kong entry ([SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/tech/tech-trends/article/3308621/robotic-hair-washer-enter-hong-kong-market-june-after-gaining-interest-mainland)), and a Toronto head spa charges C$29 ([NOW Toronto](https://nowtoronto.com/lifestyle/ai-powered-head-spas-are-here-richmond-hills-sakura-hours-offers-a-futuristic-shampoo-and-massage-experience/)). But customers complain about quality ([36Kr](https://www.36kr.com/p/3903626727786371)), and a July 2026 assessment called the stores "more gimmick than practical value" ([PEdaily](https://news.pedaily.cn/202607/566580.shtml)). The closest Australian items are staffed head spas and a plumbing-free head-spa basin sold at A$4,200 ([Harvey Norman](https://www.harveynorman.com.au/the-online-beauty-warehouse-sakura-deluxe-japanese-head-spa-no-plumbing-black.html)). The idea needs a second check and better evidence that customers are satisfied.

**Mobile home bathing for bed-bound older people** was also checked once (**absent at 75%**). ## How the evidence was gathered, and where it is thin

About 60 research, screening and verification agents ran roughly 1,700 web searches over several rounds. Ten first-round discovery sweeps covered regions (China, Southeast Asia and India; Japan and Korea; Europe, the UK and Israel; and US consumer) and sectors (mobility, robotics and logistics; climate, energy and circular; fintech, insurtech and proptech; health and care; vertical AI; and Australian tailwinds). A per-round cap of 200 searches, shared by every agent, starved several early agents, and the Europe, health and Australian-tailwinds sweeps ran no searches at all, so their scopes were re-run as three second-round discovery passes covering recent breakouts, East Asia and Europe. Three screening passes then tested about 24 more concepts. Each finalist went to two independent verifiers and then to a final confirmation agent that saw no earlier notes, and a further targeted check covered home nail robots. Two launch briefs worked out the economics with different exchange-rate assumptions: US$1 ≈ A$1.44 for the meal-robot and exoskeleton figures, and US$1 ≈ A$1.54 for the wellness figures.

The evidence is thin in five places. Network policy blocked direct page fetching throughout, so every fact comes from search-result extracts, and **every URL should be opened before it is relied on**. Four verification checks (AI bill negotiation, home warranties, remote avatar staffing and unstaffed micro-stores) ran zero searches and were not re-run, so those concepts are neither cleared nor rejected, and two more (industrial steam and vehicle scanners) ran only 2 and 5 searches. Closed or poorly indexed channels, namely WeChat, Xiaohongshu, Instagram, TikTok, Facebook groups and Google Maps, are the main blind spot, which matters most for ideas that suit Chinese-Australian operators, such as massage robots, unstaffed mahjong rooms and IPL. Absence can never be proven, and verifier confidence ranged from 65% to 85%. Finally, every price, unit-economics figure and capital figure in this report is a researcher estimate built from cited inputs.

Before launch, a founder should run direct IP Australia, ASIC and ABN searches for the concept and for each overseas leader (CHEF, Aescape, Capsix, Yiqi Future, Umia and Hypershell), and check WIPO's Madrid Monitor for international marks that designate Australia. The founder should also sweep social media and maps manually in English and Chinese, talk to suppliers about exclusivity, warranty and Australian service, and get legal advice on the TGA's intended-purpose test for exoskeletons and massage robots and on state rules for IPL and manicures. For IPL, written insurer sign-off for customer-operated devices must come before any lease. In every case, a paid pilot with a real host or customer should come before raising the full amount.

## Conclusion

Australia's missing businesses are not missing because of technology. They are missing because each needs someone in Australia to own the fleet, hold the permits, service the machines and carry the liability, and the overseas leaders are fully occupied at home. Software and hardware arrive within a year or two, but operated fleets do not. That moves the moat from code to operations: host contracts, supplier exclusivity, insurance arrangements and safety records. It also changes who the real competitors are. The likeliest fast followers are not copycat start-ups but incumbents next door, such as an integrator like A&RT/Foodmach, an attraction buying its own exoskeletons, a laser chain adding self-service rooms, or an overseas leader like 10Beauty arriving through a big retailer.

The first contracts a founder signs should therefore lock in supply and placement, meaning an exclusive supplier and an exclusive host, before any lease or consumer launch. The failures carry a second lesson. Aescape's insolvency and ONESELF's stall show that these ventures break on the business model far more often than on demand: expensive hardware on fixed rent is the trap, and cheap hardware on a host revenue share is the escape. For the three ideas that carry real physical risk (exoskeletons, massage and IPL), the winners will be the operators that treat safety evidence and insurability as the product rather than as compliance overhead.
