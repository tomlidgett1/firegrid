## 1. Quote shopping: how often Australian households switch or shop (insurance, health, energy, mortgages, telco, cars)

### Takeaway
Each quote-shopping category is roughly annual, and actual shopping rates are low. About 78% of general-insurance renewals happen without the customer approaching any other insurer, and only about 4% switch. Only 19–27% of electricity customers are on their retailer's best plan. Refinancing is the exception: it is large and active, with about 66,600 owner-occupier loans moving lender in a single quarter. Across categories a household faces roughly 7–12 renewal or shopping moments a year, but it acts on only a few unless something prompts it.

### Cited Findings
**General insurance (car, home)**
- Roy Morgan data: 78.4% of general insurance policies (38.3 million) were renewed with the same company without approaching any other company, and only 4.2% (2.1 million) were moved to a different insurer. The figure is undated in the snippets — [Mozo](https://mozo.com.au/insurance/car-insurance/articles/why-aren-t-aussies-comparing-insurance-companies-at-renewal-time); [Savings.com.au, "Australians more likely to get divorced than switch insurers"](https://www.savings.com.au/news/australians-more-likely-to-get-divorced-than-switch-insurers) (I could not tell from the snippets which of these two pages carries the Roy Morgan figure).
- "78% of Aussies don't compare home or car insurance" (Mozo headline, undated) — [Mozo](https://mozo.com.au/insurance/car-insurance/articles/78-of-aussies-don-t-compare-home-or-car-insurance-miss-out-on-savings)
- ASIC (2026, media release 26-189MR / Report 838): 68% of car insurance customers don't contact their insurer at renewal. Of those who do, 31% get a lower premium. 54% didn't know about, or missed in the fine print, the extra cost of paying by instalments rather than annually. Motor premiums rose 8% in the 12 months to July 2025 — [ASIC 26-189MR](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-189mr-consumers-left-in-the-dark-about-rising-car-insurance-premiums-asic-warns)
- Canstar survey (April 2025): 48% of drivers took steps since their last renewal to cut car insurance costs. Of those, 30% switched insurer and 32% moved to paying annually — [Canstar](https://www.canstar.com.au/car-insurance/aussies-top-tactics-to-save-on-car-insurance/)
- Finder: nearly 3 in 10 Australians changed their home and contents policy in the past two years (date unclear in snippet) — [Insurance News / Finder](https://insurancenews.com.au/local/rising-costs-force-homeowners-to-switch-ditch-cover-finder)

**Private health insurance**
- Private Health Insurance Ombudsman 2024–25 report, as reported by ACM regional papers, gives fund-level two-year member loss (cancellations plus transfers): CBHS Corporate 32.4%, Latrobe 25.4%, HIF 23.8%, NIB 21%, GMHBA 20.1%. The Ombudsman says cancellation complaints mostly reflect people moving between funds rather than leaving private cover — [Newcastle Herald (ACM)](https://www.newcastleherald.com.au/story/9207936/members-flee-private-health-insurers-as-premiums-tipped-to-rise/)
- Pureprofile headline: "Why Australians stay loyal to health insurers for 10 years on average" — [Pureprofile](https://business.pureprofile.com/why-australians-stay-loyal-to-health-insurers-for-10-years-on-average)
- GMHBA's new membership policies rose 94% year on year in 2025–26 (source of members not stated) — [Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/au/news/life-insurance/gmhba-records-sharp-rise-in-new-policies-589931.aspx)

**Energy**
- ACCC December 2025 electricity inquiry report (14th report): about 27% of residential customers were on their retailer's best plan at some point in 2024–25. The share of customers on plans less than a year old rose from 29% to 42% between August 2024 and August 2025, and residential bills rose 6% over the same period (excluding rebates) — [ACCC December 2025 report](https://www.accc.gov.au/inquiries-and-consultations/electricity-market-monitoring-inquiry-2018-26/december-2025-report)
- Energy Consumers Australia, citing an earlier ACCC report: only 19% of customers were on their retailer's best offer — [ECA submission to AEMC, 31 July 2025](https://energyconsumersaustralia.com.au/our-work/submissions/submission-aemc-draft-determination-improving-ability-switch-better-offer)
- AEMC final determination (11 September 2025), "Improving the ability to switch to a better offer" (RRC0062), aims to increase switching to cheaper plans — [AEMC final determination](https://www.aemc.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-09/Final%20determination%20-%20RRC0062%20Improving%20the%20ability%20to%20switch%20to%20a%20better%20offer%20%281%29.pdf); [AEMC rule change page](https://aemc.gov.au/rule-changes/improving-ability-switch-better-offer)

**Mortgages and refinancing**
- In the March quarter 2026, owner-occupiers moved A$42.9bn across 66,617 loans to a new lender, plus A$27.3bn of internal refinancing. This comes from Mozo citing ABS Lending Indicators and was not checked against the ABS release — [Mozo refinance page](https://cdn.mozo.com.au/home-loans/refinance)
- Earlier ABS context: owner-occupier refinancing between lenders hit a then-record A$13.4bn in November 2022 — [ABS media release](https://www.abs.gov.au/media-centre/media-releases/refinancing-reached-another-record-high-november)
- Brokers wrote a record 81.6% of new residential home loans in the June 2026 quarter (A$139.08bn), up from 77.6% a year earlier and 53.9% in June 2018 (MFAA/Cotality) — [MFAA release, Sept 2026](https://www.mfaa.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/MORTGAGE-BROKERS-FACILITATE-A-RECORD-81.6-OF-ALL-NEW-HOME-LOAN-LENDING-IN-AUSTRALIA.pdf); [MPA](https://www.mpamag.com/au/news/general/broker-market-share-hits-new-record/588423)

### Inferences
- Annualising Mozo's Q1 2026 figure gives roughly 250,000–270,000 owner-occupier external refinances a year. Refinancing is the biggest-ticket and most actively shopped category, but per household it happens only every few years.
- A typical household has about 7–12 renewal or shopping moments a year: car insurance (1–2 cars), home and contents, health, electricity (plus gas), mobile (1–3 lines), NBN, pet and travel insurance, and an occasional refinance review or car purchase. That is a theoretical **0.6–1.0 per month**. Given that roughly 78% of insurance renewals involve no shopping at all, the realised rate without prompting is probably **0.1–0.25 per month per household**.
- The low base rate is the opportunity. A text-a-number service that knows each renewal date could send a message like "your car insurance renews in 3 weeks — want 3 competing quotes and a call?" That would turn latent annual events into an actual monthly cadence. ASIC's finding that 31% of people who contact their insurer get a lower premium suggests even one call is worth something to the user.
- Quote shopping has low leakage by design. Users want new competing offers each time, so there is no "regular provider" to save in their contacts. The leak instead goes to the incumbent, through auto-renewal.

### Gaps
- No national annual switching rate for electricity was found. The ACCC December 2025 report and IPART's 2024–25 NSW monitoring report contain switching data, but the snippets did not include the figure.
- No national private health insurance switching rate was confirmed. Search snippets also mentioned a Money.com.au survey (October 2025) finding 46% of policyholders have never switched and 33% have switched once, but the source URL could not be confirmed.
- No phone/NBN switching data or car purchase frequency data was found; neither was searched, because of budget.
- Unverified background: Australians typically replace a car every several years. The mobile market is dominated by Telstra, Optus and TPG/Vodafone plus many MVNOs, with month-to-month plans that make switching easy.

## 2. Quote shopping: what providers and brokers pay per lead or per customer

### Takeaway
Mortgages pay by far the most. A broker earns roughly A$3,300–4,200 upfront plus about A$900–1,200 a year in trail on a A$600k loan, and paid search costs brokers an implied A$250–375 per enquiry. Health insurance historically paid commissions of up to 50% of the first-year premium (2016 data). Solar leads trade transparently at A$35–150. No reliable public per-lead prices were found for general insurance, energy or telco referrals.

### Cited Findings
**Mortgage broking**
- Upfront commission is typically 0.55%–0.70% of the loan (A$3,300–4,200 on a A$600,000 loan). Trail is about 0.15%–0.20% a year on the outstanding balance, with some sources quoting 0.15%–0.25% — [Hunter Galloway 2026 guide](https://www.huntergalloway.com.au/mortgage-broker-fees-australia-2026-guide-for-first-home-buyers/); [Integrated Finance Group 2026](https://integratedfinancegroup.com.au/blog/how-mortgage-brokers-get-paid-honest-truth-2026/); [Peakifi 2026](https://peakifi.com/mortgages/brokers/how-brokers-are-paid/)
- Clawback applies if the loan is refinanced early: 100% within 12 months, 50% at 12–18 months and 25% at 18–24 months (the Mozo page contained editing artefacts, so treat with caution) — [Mozo broker fees guide](https://mozo.com.au/home-loans/resources/guides/mortgage-broker-fees)
- No published Australian per-lead price for home loan leads was found. One vendor (Your Finance Guide) quotes a per-lead price on request and sells postcode-matched leads to a single broker — [Your Finance Guide](https://www.yourfinanceguide.com.au/finance-leads/mortgage-broker-leads)
- The same vendor's blog puts paid-search costs at about A$20–30 per click for home loan terms, or about A$250–375 per enquiry at an assumed 8% conversion. It also claims aggregator Finsure sells raw leads in minimum lots of 50 (a vendor claim, unverified) — [Your Finance Guide blog, 2026](https://www.yourfinanceguide.com.au/blog/mortgage-broker-marketing-lead-channels-2026)
- US benchmark (2023, not Australian): mortgage lead platforms charge about US$20–60 per lead — [Omni Online Strategies](https://omnionlinestrategies.com/the-cost-of-mortgage-leads-in-2023-a-comprehensive-comparison-and-analysis)

**Health insurance**
- Compare the Market says partner health funds pay it the same fee for every referred customer who joins, regardless of fund, except for Gold hospital cover — [Compare the Market disclosures](https://www.comparethemarket.com.au/health-insurance/disclosures/)
- 2016 reporting (dated): iSelect's upfront commissions could reach 50% of the first-year premium, with trail of up to 6.5% of premiums on renewal — [The New Daily, 2016](https://thenewdaily.com.au/money/2016/03/15/private-health-costs-commissions)
- CHOICE says iSelect would not disclose specific commission details, citing confidentiality with insurer partners — [CHOICE](https://www.choice.com.au/money/insurance/insurance-advice/articles/insurance-comparison-sites)
- iSelect discloses that not all policies it compares are available to all customers, because of commercial arrangements — [iSelect disclosures](https://www.iselect.com.au/health-insurance/disclosures/); [How iSelect makes money](https://www.iselect.com.au/help-centre/how-iselect-makes-money/)

**Solar and battery (comparable to quote-shopping leads)**
- SolarQuotes charges A$45 + GST per shared lead with a 7-day rejection window. Across sites, installers pay roughly A$30–150 per lead — [WhySolar](https://www.whysolar.com.au/blog/solar-quote-comparison-sites-australia)
- Solar Calculator charges A$40 + GST for solar and battery leads, A$36 + GST for battery-only and A$45 + GST for off-grid, with no commission — [Solar Calculator](https://solarcalculator.com.au/installer-registration/)
- Solar Proof's marketplace charges A$35 for a shared lead and A$120 for an exclusive lead (promotional page) — [Solar Proof](https://www.solarproof.com.au/leads/index.php)
- A trade lead seller's claim (competitor marketing): aggregators charge A$150–300 a month plus A$50–100 per shared lead — [What's The Damage](https://whatsthedamage.com.au/tradies/solar/melbourne/)

**Energy**
- Commercial energy comparison sites earn a commission or referral fee from retailers while advertising themselves as "100% free". A 2017-era analysis found offers through commercial sites were 5–12% dearer than retailers' direct offers (dated) — [RenewEconomy](https://reneweconomy.com.au/energy-price-gouging-exposed-by-new-regulations-34333/); [Solar Directory repost](https://www.solardirectory.com.au/news/2117-energy-price-gouging)

### Inferences
- One settled refinance (about A$3.3–4.2k upfront plus trail) is worth more to a broker than an entire year of insurance, energy and telco referrals for that household. A broker could rationally pay A$150–400 for a qualified, consented refinance or pre-approval callback, since that is the range Google implies. Even at a 1-in-5 settlement rate, the broker keeps a large margin.
- Health insurance is the second-richest category. Even if commissions have fallen since 2016, a first-year commission in the hundreds of dollars per family policy is plausible, but I could not verify it.
- Solar lead prices (A$35–150) are a useful floor for a consented, phone-verified household lead in a non-financial category. They are probably also the ceiling for energy and telco.
- Lead value is inversely related to regulatory ease. The categories that pay the most (credit, health, insurance) are the most regulated (see section 4).

### Gaps
- No current figures were found for per-referral fees for general insurance (car, home), energy switches, or phone/NBN plans.
- No current (post-2016) health insurance commission rates were found.
- No current average loan size was verified (the A$600k example comes from broker marketing).
- Unverified background: energy comparison sites have historically been paid per completed switch, and telco comparison sites earn modest per-connection fees. Car finance brokers earn commissions from lenders. None of these were verified.

## 3. Quote shopping: existing players, models and consumer frustration with comparison sites

### Takeaway
The market is crowded: Compare the Market, iSelect, Canstar, Finder and Mozo on the comparison side, a broker channel that already writes 81.6% of home loans, and free government comparators for energy. Consumers' central complaint is being called immediately and repeatedly after entering details, because their data is resold. Regulators confirm this resale is widespread. That pain maps directly onto a "you text first, one chosen human calls you, your number stays masked" design.

### Cited Findings
- Trustpilot reviews of Compare the Market Australia (about 6,000 reviews) describe calls about five minutes after using a quote wizard, repeated calls after asking for a result, and calls continuing after the person asked them to stop — [Trustpilot (nl-be)](https://nl-be.trustpilot.com/review/www.comparethemarket.com.au); [Trustpilot (it), page 3](https://it.trustpilot.com/review/www.comparethemarket.com.au?page=3)
- A regulator spokesperson warned that personal details entered on comparison sites are sometimes sold, which can lead to telemarketing calls; the article is headlined "Apples and oranges: watchdog's comparison site warning" — [Canberra Times (ACM)](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/9354725/apples-and-oranges-watchdogs-comparison-site-warning/); [Newcastle Herald](https://www.newcastleherald.com.au/story/9354725/apples-and-oranges-watchdogs-comparison-site-warning/)
- The ACCC found that consumers' personal data is routinely collected through price comparison websites, online quote tools, free trials and surveys, then sold through data brokers and used to start unsolicited sales contacts — [ACCC media release](https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/misleading-and-high-pressure-unsolicited-sales-practices-widespread); [Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/au/news/breaking-news/comparison-sites-and-data-brokers-caught-in-acccs-unsolicited-selling-net-583947.aspx)
- ASIC alleges (September 2026) that Clark Family Pty Ltd ran up to 70 "free assessment" websites for loans and insurance between September 2020 and September 2026. According to ASIC, the sites did no comparison and sold each enquiry through an internal bidding process to the highest-bidding broker, who then called the consumer. These are allegations only — [ASIC 26-221MR](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-221mr-asic-sues-lead-generation-business-over-alleged-misleading-comparison-claims/); [ASIC concise statement](https://download.asic.gov.au/media/jyrlaeuh/26-221mr-concise-statement.pdf); [The Adviser](https://www.theadviser.com.au/compliance/48970-asic-sues-business-for-alleged-misleading-loan-comparisons)
- Insurance Business reported ASIC action over an "allegedly misleading insurance lead model" (Insure 247), whose websites gave the impression of a comparison tool but did no evaluation. The snippets did not make clear whether this is part of the Clark Family proceedings — [Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/au/news/breaking-news/asic-takes-legal-action-over-allegedly-misleading-insurance-lead-model-590442.aspx)
- The Federal Court found that comparison site Choosi misled consumers over about four years from mid-2019 (attribution via search summary) — [riskinfo](https://riskinfo.com.au/news/?p=23317)
- The Consumer Utilities Advocacy Centre found energy comparison sites a "pot-luck" for consumers. The cheapest product on Victorian Energy Compare did not appear on any commercial comparison site (older report) — [CUAC via AER](https://www.aer.gov.au/system/files/Consumer%20Utilities%20Advocacy%20Centre%20-%20Attachment%201_0.pdf)
- Finder's CEO said its energy ranking puts relevance ahead of price, weighing popularity, fees, flexibility and green options — [Wattever](https://wattever.com.au/?p=19397) (snippet attribution; original ABC Everyday statement not retrieved)
- A comparison guide reports that Reddit users often advise using a throwaway email or number because "the phone may ring a lot" (secondhand, US-oriented page) — [Gerald](https://joingerald.com/learn/life--lifestyle/smarter-auto-insurance-review-2026)

### Inferences
- Three things set the founder's product apart: (a) the user explicitly asks for a call, (b) one or a few vetted providers call rather than a data auction, and (c) masked numbers prevent resale or repeat calls. That is the inverse of the behaviour regulators are attacking in 2025–26, and it is a credible "no spam" brand promise.
- Comparison sites already monetise the same categories at scale and outspend on brand. A solo founder will not win a head-to-head "compare" fight. Better positions are the trusted callback layer, or a partner to one licensed broker per vertical.
- Because brokers write 81.6% of home loans, "find me a broker" is not an unsolved problem. The unsolved part is trust and not being spammed. Pitch it as "a vetted broker calls you, once, and your number never leaks".

### Gaps
- No Reddit or Whirlpool threads were retrieved directly (no search hits). The consumer-frustration evidence comes from Trustpilot and press.
- No investor or market-share data was gathered for Canstar, Finder, iSelect, Compare the Market, Mozo, Econnex, Lendi or Aussie.
- Unverified background: Compare the Market is owned by Auto & General (the Budget Direct group); a Trustpilot reviewer raised this ownership, but it was not verified. iSelect was taken private in about 2022. Lendi and Aussie combined into Lendi Group in about 2021–22. Energy Made Easy (AER) is the free government energy comparator outside Victoria, and Victorian Energy Compare covers Victoria. privatehealth.gov.au is the government health-insurance comparator.

## 4. Quote shopping: Australian regulation (ASIC, ACCC, Treasury, licensing, unfair trading, energy comparison)

### Takeaway
As of October 2026, lead generation for financial products is the most intensely scrutinised consumer-marketing practice in Australia. Treasury issued a consultation paper in April 2026 proposing licensee accountability for lead generators, and ASIC's lead-generation watchlist had grown to 63 entities by June 2026. ASIC sued an alleged lead-auction comparison operator in September 2026, the ACCC's July 2026 report recommends explicit unsolicited-selling rules for digital lead generation, and a general unfair-trading prohibition starts on 1 July 2027. For credit, the referrer exemption probably does NOT cover a business whose main activity is passing on leads, which describes this product. A solo founder would need to become a credit representative or authorised representative of a licensed partner, or obtain their own licence.

### Cited Findings
**Treasury lead-generation consultation (2026)**
- Treasury released a consultation paper on 7 April 2026 asking for feedback on policy options to curb lead generation linked to financial products. It responds to the collapse of the Shield and First Guardian master funds — [Mondaq / HSF Kramer submission](https://www.mondaq.com/australia/financial-services/1799200/hsf-kramer-submission-treasury-consultation-on-curbing-lead-generation-activity); [HSF Kramer](https://www.hsfkramer.com/fr/notes/fsraustralia/2026-posts/hsf-kramer-submission-treasury-consultation-on-curbing-lead-generation-activity)
- Trade press describes the paper as proposing to make licensees more accountable for the conduct of the lead generators they use — [Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/au/news/breaking-news/comparison-sites-and-data-brokers-caught-in-acccs-unsolicited-selling-net-583947.aspx)
- HSF Kramer supports tougher rules on harmful lead generation but warns some proposals could catch legitimate marketing. It argues that targeted enforcement against unlicensed lead generators and defective advisers would have reduced the harm — [Mondaq / HSF Kramer](https://www.mondaq.com/australia/financial-services/1799200/hsf-kramer-submission-treasury-consultation-on-curbing-lead-generation-activity)

**ASIC**
- On 18 February 2026, ASIC began reviewing advice licensees that use lead-generation services, focused on superannuation switching. It published lists of 21 lead generators or referral partners and 23 advice licensees or corporate authorised representatives that acquired leads since 1 July 2024. ASIC said listing implies no wrongdoing, and warned that lead generators that mislead, use high-pressure tactics or provide financial services without a licence risk breaking the law — [Adviser Voice](https://www.adviservoice.com.au/2026/02/asic-commences-new-review-of-advice-licensees-that-use-lead-generation-services/); [SMS Magazine](https://smsmagazine.com.au/news/2026/02/18/asic-to-examine-lead-generated-advice/); [ASIC financial advice update Feb 2026](https://asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/news-items/financial-advice-update-february-2026/)
- ASIC later expanded the list (media release 26-125MR, "ASIC expands list of known entities involved in lead generation"). Trade press reports 19 entities added, for 63 in total by June 2026, up from 44 — [ASIC 26-125MR](https://asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-125mr-asic-expands-list-of-known-entities-involved-in-lead-generation/); [Financial Newswire](https://financialnewswire.com.au/financial-planning/asic-adds-19-entities-to-lead-gen-list/)
- ASIC v Clark Family (September 2026) alleges breaches of ASIC Act ss 12DB and 12DF (misleading representations) through lead-auction "comparison" sites for loans and insurance. ASIC seeks penalties, an adverse publicity order and injunctions — [ASIC 26-221MR](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-221mr-asic-sues-lead-generation-business-over-alleged-misleading-comparison-claims/)
- ASIC's longstanding position (2012): websites that let consumers get or compare insurance quotes are generally providing financial services, so they must hold an AFSL or be an authorised representative of a licensee. They should also disclose links to providers, commissions, referral fees and payments for featured placement — [ASIC 12-304MR](https://asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2012-releases/12-304mr-asic-warns-comparison-websites)

**Credit referral licensing (relevant to mortgage, refinance and car-finance callbacks)**
- The referrer exemption sits in reg 25(5) of the National Consumer Credit Protection Regulations 2010. The referrer must not charge the consumer, must have the consumer's consent to pass on their details, and must have an agreement with the licensee. Commission may be paid, but the licensee must keep a register, and website referrals require commission disclosure under reg 25(2A) — [MFAA referrer exemption guide (Feb 2025)](https://portal.mfaa.com.au/broker-toolbox/legal-and-compliance/nccp-bid/Referrer%20exemption%20File); [Brightlaw](https://www.brightlaw.com.au/new-credit-licensing-exemptions-for-referrers-and-temporary-employees/); [Westpac referrer guidelines](https://westpac.com.au/content/dam/public/wbc/documents/pdf/pb/home-loans/referrer-guidelines.pdf)
- Key limit: the referral must be incidental to a business that is not principally about contacting people to pass their details to others. Call centres doing marketing may therefore need a licence or appointment as a credit representative — [MFAA guide](https://portal.mfaa.com.au/broker-toolbox/legal-and-compliance/nccp-bid/Referrer%20exemption%20File); [AFSL House](https://afslhouse.com.au/insights/australian-credit-licence-exemptions/)

**ACCC: unsolicited selling and lead generation**
- The review began in June 2025 after a designated complaint from the Consumer Action Law Centre in March 2025. The report, dated 28 July 2026 by trade press, recommends: bringing digital lead generation explicitly within the unsolicited-selling rules; higher penalties; an "opt-in" model for consumers targeted by unsolicited sales; and support for privacy reforms. Current maximum penalties are A$50,000 for a corporation and A$10,000 for an individual. The ACCC's untested view is that a sale stays "unsolicited" unless the purpose of collecting the data (to generate a sales contact) was clearly disclosed — [ACCC media release](https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/misleading-and-high-pressure-unsolicited-sales-practices-widespread); [Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/au/news/breaking-news/comparison-sites-and-data-brokers-caught-in-acccs-unsolicited-selling-net-583947.aspx); [ACCC consultation page](https://consultation.accc.gov.au/accc/unsolicited-selling-and-lead-generation-review/)
- The Consumer Action Law Centre called it a "blistering report on harms" that "falls short on banning 'unsolicited selling'" — [CALC](https://consumeraction.org.au/accc-releases-blistering-report-on-harms-but-falls-short-on-banning-unsolicited-selling/)

**Unfair trading practices prohibition (from 2027)**
- The Competition and Consumer Amendment (Unfair Trading Practices) Bill 2026 passed Parliament on 2 July 2026, and its main provisions start on 1 July 2027. It prohibits conduct that manipulates consumers or unreasonably distorts their decision-making and is likely to cause detriment, which can include wasted time and frustration. It also adds subscription rules (upfront disclosure, removing cancellation barriers, coverage of free or discounted trials and of existing contracts renewed after commencement) and drip-pricing rules — [Allens, July 2026](https://www.allens.com.au/insights-news/insights/2026/07/australias-new-unfair-trading-practices-regime-what-businesses-need-to-know/); [HWL Ebsworth](https://hwlebsworth.com.au/acl-updates-new-unfair-trading-practices-laws-to-apply-from-2027/); [Hall & Wilcox](https://hallandwilcox.com.au/news/new-consumer-law-reforms-for-unfair-trading-practices-pricing-and-subscription-contracts/)
- Sources disagree on penalties. Mondaq gives the greater of A$50m, 3x the benefit, or 30% of adjusted turnover, while Ashurst and Marsdens cite A$100m — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/australia/consumer-trading-unfair-trading/1753738/businesses-face-new-compliance-hurdles-under-proposed-unfair-trading-laws); [Ashurst](https://www.ashurstperkinscoie.com/en/insights/australia-bans-unfair-trading-practices-strengthens-laws-against-drip-pricing-and-subscription-traps/); [Marsdens](https://www.marsdens.net.au/about-us/latest-news/unfair-trading-practices-bill-2026-australia/)

**Energy comparison**
- In Victoria, retailers must enter every offer into the Victorian Retail Portal, which feeds Victorian Energy Compare, the government's free comparator, and must provide a standard Victorian Energy Fact Sheet (ESC, 2019) — [ESC draft fact sheet guidelines (2019)](https://www.esc.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/draft-energy-fact-sheet-guidelines-20190312.pdf); [ESC technical final decision (2019)](https://mail.esc.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/technical-final-decision-on-the-victorian-energy-fact-sheet-20190508_0.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Credit (mortgage, refinance, car finance) is the most valuable and the most constrained.** A text-a-number service whose main activity is routing people to brokers is very likely outside the reg 25 referrer exemption. Realistic routes are appointment as a credit representative of a licensed broker or aggregator, or a white-label arrangement where the partner broker is the licensee of record. Treasury's direction, licensees accountable for their lead generators, means partner brokers will do heavier due diligence and want consent records, scripts and call logs. The founder's architecture (explicit opt-in by text, logged, masked callback) suits that.
- **Insurance comparison or quote-getting is a "financial service".** The founder would need to be an authorised representative of an AFSL holder, such as an insurance broker, and restrict the agent to factual information or general advice. Getting quotes on someone's behalf is closer to "arranging" than a simple referral.
- **ACCC opt-in recommendation:** a service the consumer starts by texting is opt-in by construction. The risk is any onward sale or sharing of the user's data beyond the provider they chose, which the design should prohibit.
- **From 1 July 2027**, any JustAnswer-style teaser subscription or hard-to-cancel model is high risk in Australia. That affects the expert family (section 6) more than quote shopping.
- **Energy and telco carry the least regulatory burden** of the quote categories: no AFSL or ACL is needed. They still fall within ACCC unsolicited-selling and energy marketing rules, and they compete with free government comparators.

### Gaps
- The Treasury consultation paper itself, with its specific options, scope (super only, or insurance and credit too) and submission deadline, was not retrieved. The only primary-adjacent source is HSF Kramer commentary.
- ASIC's February 2026 review findings had not been published as of the search results. The figure of 63 entities comes from trade press.
- Whether the government has responded to the ACCC unsolicited-selling recommendations with draft legislation was not found.
- No current rules were found on commercial energy comparator commission disclosure in Victoria or the NEM.
- Unverified background: private health insurance is regulated mainly under the Private Health Insurance Act and the PHI Ombudsman, rather than the Corporations Act's AFSL regime, which could make health-fund referral less burdensome than general insurance. Energy switching requires "explicit informed consent" under the National Energy Retail Law. Both should be confirmed with a lawyer.

## 5. Real-estate buyer and renter journeys

### Takeaway
The search found no quantitative Australian data on how many agents a buyer contacts per week, or on callback rates. Agent non-responsiveness is a recurring complaint, mostly from US agent blogs plus one Australian trade outlet. The monetisable pieces are clear and high-value: a mortgage pre-approval lead for a broker (about A$3.3–4.2k upfront commission per settled A$600k loan) and buyer's agent engagements (A$6k–30k fixed or 1.5–3%).

### Cited Findings
- Buyer's agent fees (2026 guides): full-search fixed fees of about A$6,000–12,000 under about A$1m and A$12,000–30,000 for premium purchases. Typical capital-city full service costs A$10k–25k, and percentage models charge 1.5%–3% of the purchase price (another source: 1.7%–3% + GST). Auction bidding only costs about A$500–1,500, and due diligence about A$500–1,000 per property — [What's The Damage](https://whatsthedamage.com.au/buyers-agent-cost/); [Your Property Guide (reviewed May 2026)](https://www.yourpropertyguide.com.au/guides/buyers-agent-cost-australia); [Property Update](https://propertyupdate.com.au/how-much-do-buyers-agents-charge-in-australia/)
- Elite Agent (Australian real-estate trade outlet) reports that failing to return phone calls during a negotiation, and failing to respond to buyers after an offer, were key buyer complaints — [Elite Agent](https://eliteagent.com/?p=87261)
- Industry blogs argue that agents miss buyer calls and buyers "don't ring twice" (non-Australian/generic) — [Callyzer](https://callyzer.co/blog/missed-calls-in-real-estate-sales/); a 2004 US column describes an informal poll of agents in which responsiveness was "Not always" and "Almost never" — [Inman (2004)](https://inman.com/2004/12/10/unresponsive-home-seller-syndrome)
- Mortgage pre-approval value: see the broker commission figures in section 2 — [Hunter Galloway](https://www.huntergalloway.com.au/mortgage-broker-fees-australia-2026-guide-for-first-home-buyers/); brokers wrote 81.6% of new home loans in the June 2026 quarter — [MFAA](https://www.mfaa.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/MORTGAGE-BROKERS-FACILITATE-A-RECORD-81.6-OF-ALL-NEW-HOME-LOAN-LENDING-IN-AUSTRALIA.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The house hunt is a burst of high frequency with low leakage.** Every listing has a different selling agent, so during a 3–6 month search the user really does need a new human repeatedly, possibly several times a week. That fits the platform-frequency definition well, but it happens once every several years per household.
- **The mechanic changes.** The provider is fixed by the listing, so the agent's job is to chase and confirm ("get the selling agent of 12 Smith St to call me about price guide and the contract") rather than to match. Selling agents want buyers, so callbacks should be easy. The pain is speed and follow-through, not discovery.
- **Monetisation is indirect but rich.** The callback itself has little paying value, but the same conversation surfaces "are you pre-approved?" (a broker lead worth hundreds of dollars), "want a buyer's agent?" (a A$10k–25k engagement) and conveyancer or building-inspection referrals. Credit referral licensing (section 4) applies to the pre-approval leg.
- **Renters:** no evidence was found that callbacks are the bottleneck. Rental applications run through portals and inspections, so this probably has low fit.

### Gaps
- No Australian data on the number of agents contacted or inspections attended per week, agent callback rates, average search duration, or how often households move. No Reddit (r/AusPropertyChat) evidence was retrieved.
- No data on buyer's agent referral fees paid to introducers.
- Renter-journey pain points were not researched (budget).

## 6. Expert advice on demand: JustAnswer, other platforms and willingness to pay

### Takeaway
JustAnswer proves that breadth works as a model: about 12,000 experts across 700 categories, and a US$100m gross revenue run-rate as early as 2012, with 80% of questions in doctors, lawyers, mechanics and tech support. But its monetisation is under attack. The FTC sued it in January 2026 over US$1–5 "join" offers that turned into US$28–125 monthly subscriptions. Australia's 2027 unfair-trading law targets the same tactic. In Australia, free public lines compete in several big domains, and per-question willingness to pay looks low without a subscription hook.

### Cited Findings
**JustAnswer**
- Current scale: about 12K experts, 700 categories and 196 countries; founder and CEO Andy Kurtzig — [JustAnswer About](https://www.justanswer.com/info/about); [JustAnswer UK About](https://www.justanswer.co.uk/about)
- 2012: gross revenue run-rate of US$100m. Of 700 categories, 80% of questions were answered by doctors, lawyers, mechanics and tech support (Kurtzig) — [Vator, 2012](https://vator.tv/news/2012-06-19-justanswer-rebrands-as-pearl-raises-25m)
- Earlier scale claims: about 20,000 experts across 90 categories (2009) — [Google Partners blog](https://agency.googleblog.com/2009/05/new-success-story-justanswer-finds.html); raised more than US$50m in financing (date not in snippet) — [Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/starting-a-business/online-resource-for-expert-advice-lines-up-more-than-50/226749)
- Pricing: aggregator profiles cite a US subscription of about US$60 a month (Simplify says US$65) plus pay-per-question — [Simplify](https://simplify.jobs/c/JustAnswer); [Spain startup directory](https://startups.one.gob.es/companies/justanswer_com). UK vet category priced at £27 a month as of 1 October 2022 (dated) — [JustAnswer UK pricing](https://www.justanswer.co.uk/help/pricing)
- FTC lawsuit, filed 13 January 2026 in N.D. Cal. against JustAnswer LLC and CEO Andrew Kurtzig. It alleges a US$1 or US$5 "join" offer enrolled consumers in monthly subscriptions of about US$28–125 that kept billing until cancelled, with confusing checkout flows and buried disclosures. The claims are under ROSCA and FTC Act s5, seeking injunctions, refunds and civil penalties. JustAnswer said it was "disappointed" and expects to resolve the matter. One law blog mentions a proposed settlement order, which I could not verify — [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/327857); [Fox Rothschild](https://advertisinglaw.foxrothschild.com/2026/02/ftc-sues-justanswer-for-deceptive-subscription-practices/); [Subscription Insider](https://www.subscriptioninsider.com/article-type/news/ftc-sues-justanswer-over-low-cost-join-offer-that-allegedly-led-to-monthly-subscriptions); [AARP](https://www.aarp.org/money/scams-fraud/justanswer-accused-of-subscription-traps/)
- PPC Land headline: "FTC: JustAnswer trapped consumers in hidden subscriptions that cost 26 times the advertised price" — [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/ftc-justanswer-trapped-consumers-in-hidden-subscriptions-that-cost-26-times-the-advertised-price/)

**Other expert-call platforms (US precedents)**
- Clarity.fm: experts set an hourly rate, which is converted to a per-minute charge and billed on actual call length, with no pricing guidelines enforced — [Clarity help](https://clarity.fm/help/articles/23/how-are-rates-for-experts-determined-on-clarity). Its take rate was about 15% per an older (~2020) community answer — [LaunchRock community](https://www.launchrock.com/community/questions/6868/how-much-do-clarify-fm-pays-an-expert-for-answering-a-question). A 2026 competitor page says "15–20%+" for Clarity and 25–30% for Intro.co (competitor source, treat with caution) — [Talkspresso](https://talkspresso.com/blog/intro-co-vs-clarity-fm-2026)

**Australian legal advice**
- LawPath (third-party listings, unverified against LawPath): Essentials A$99 a month billed monthly or A$29 a month billed annually; Legal Advice plan A$89 a month; Legal Partner A$599 a month — [GetApp AU](https://www.getapp.com.au/software/2048369/legal-advice-plan); [Capterra AU](https://www.capterra.com.au/software/203558/legal-advice-plan)
- A LawPath partner promo offers "unlimited legal documents from $29/month* and unlimited 30 minute calls with a lawyer" (new-customer offer) — [LawPath x GoDaddy](https://try.lawpath.com.au/partner/godaddy)
- LawPath arranges fixed-price quotes from a "1000+ network of expert lawyers", and subscriptions can be cancelled at any time from the Settings tab — [LawPath FAQ](https://lawpath.com.au/faq)

### Inferences
- **Breadth creates frequency on paper, but the "new human" share is lower than it looks.** Accountants, vets and mechanics are sticky relationships (high leakage once found). Lawyers are needed infrequently. Medical questions in Australia go to free government lines (see gaps). The domains where a new person is genuinely needed each time are those where the user has no relationship and the question is urgent: tech troubleshooting, one-off legal questions, a car problem on the road, after-hours pet worries.
- **JustAnswer's economics rely on subscription capture.** The FTC complaint describes billing of up to 26 times the advertised price. A clean Australian replica, with no teaser and easy cancellation as required from July 2027, would earn far less per user. That suggests per-question willingness to pay is weak: people pay US$1–5 to ask, not US$60.
- **The LawPath precedent** (about A$29–99 a month with lawyer calls) shows Australians will pay a subscription for legal access, but mostly small businesses, not households.
- **Per-call versus subscription:** a callback model maps naturally to per-call pricing (Clarity-style per minute, take rate 15–30%). Subscription is the only proven way to make breadth pay, and it is now a regulatory hazard. A hybrid with no auto-renew trap (for example a prepaid "call credit" pack or a family plan with explicit renewal consent) fits the 2027 rules.

### Gaps
- No evidence of JustAnswer's Australian presence, Australian pricing or customer numbers was found. Trustpilot and ProductReview complaint volumes were not retrieved.
- JustAnswer's current revenue was not found (the latest is the 2012 run-rate).
- Australian legal advice lines (Legal Aid state hotlines, LawAccess NSW, community legal centres, Law Society referral services), accountants' Q&A services, and vet or mechanic advice lines were not researched (budget).
- Unverified background: healthdirect runs a free 24/7 nurse advice line (1800 022 222). State Legal Aid commissions and LawAccess NSW provide free legal information lines. Under the Legal Profession Uniform Law, only practising lawyers may give legal advice, and in Australia only registered tax agents may give tax-agent services for a fee. Each means an AI-only answer is restricted and a human professional is required, which suits the callback architecture.

## 8. Family-level evaluation and ranked scoring table

### Takeaway
Quote shopping has the money (credit, health) and naturally low leakage. However, as of October 2026 it is Australia's most actively policed lead-generation space, and its realised frequency per household is only about 0.1–0.25 a month unless the product proactively prompts renewals. Expert-advice breadth looks frequent but leaks into sticky relationships and free public lines, and JustAnswer-style monetisation is under legal attack. The best-scoring individual candidates are (1) family-paid tech help for older parents, (2) the house-hunt "make the agent call me back" burst, with mortgage pre-approval and buyer's agent referrals as the monetisation, and (3) a proactive multi-category renewal concierge. The concierge scores high on frequency and gap, but is dragged down by licensing.

### Cited Findings
- Insurance shopping rates are low (78.4% renew without approaching others; 4.2% switch) — [Mozo](https://mozo.com.au/insurance/car-insurance/articles/why-aren-t-aussies-comparing-insurance-companies-at-renewal-time)
- Broker economics: 0.55–0.70% upfront plus 0.15–0.20% trail — [Hunter Galloway](https://www.huntergalloway.com.au/mortgage-broker-fees-australia-2026-guide-for-first-home-buyers/)
- Solar leads: A$35–150 — [Solar Proof](https://www.solarproof.com.au/leads/index.php); [WhySolar](https://www.whysolar.com.au/blog/solar-quote-comparison-sites-australia)
- Regulatory heat: Treasury consultation (April 2026), ASIC watchlist (63 by June 2026), ASIC v Clark Family (September 2026), ACCC report (July 2026), unfair trading practices law (from July 2027) — see section 4 sources, e.g. [ASIC 26-221MR](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-221mr-asic-sues-lead-generation-business-over-alleged-misleading-comparison-claims/); [Allens](https://www.allens.com.au/insights-news/insights/2026/07/australias-new-unfair-trading-practices-regime-what-businesses-need-to-know/)
- JustAnswer: 80% of questions in four domains; FTC subscription suit — [Vator](https://vator.tv/news/2012-06-19-justanswer-rebrands-as-pearl-raises-25m); [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/327857)
- Seniors' in-home tech help costs A$80–150 an hour — [ACT Seniors Card directory](https://www.actseniorscard.org.au/index.php/act-seniors-card-discount-directory/category/computers-and-computer-support-2)

### Inferences

**Family 1: Quote shopping (summary)**
- Frequency per household per month: potential 0.6–1.0 across categories, realised about 0.1–0.25 without prompting (inference from section 1).
- Leakage: low (users want new competitors each time). The main leak is passive auto-renewal with the incumbent.
- Lead value: mortgage (very high, hundreds of dollars per lead and thousands per settlement); health (high, but 2016 data); solar (A$35–150); general insurance, energy and telco (unknown, probably lower).
- Solution gap: crowded on "compare", open on "no-spam, consented, one human calls you" (sections 3–4).
- Regulatory risk: high for credit and insurance (credit representative or authorised representative needed; Treasury and ASIC lead-generation focus). Moderate for health. Lower for energy and telco.
- Solo-founder feasibility: best as a thin consent-and-callback layer on top of one licensed partner per vertical (one broker, one insurance broker, one energy broker), not as a comparison engine.

**Family 2: Expert advice on demand (summary)**
- Frequency per household per month: maybe 0.3–0.8 "questions" with breadth, but only about 0.1–0.3 needing a NEW human, because accountants, vets and mechanics are sticky (inference).
- Leakage: medium to high for relationship domains; low for urgent one-off domains (tech, roadside car issues, one-off legal).
- Willingness to pay: weak per question (JustAnswer's US$1–5 entry point; its revenue relies on subscriptions). LawPath shows about A$29–99 a month for legal access, mostly from businesses.
- Solution gap: JustAnswer's AU presence was not verified. Free public lines cover health, legal information and digital literacy. The gap is "a human calls you now", not information.
- Regulatory risk: professional-practice restrictions (law, tax, financial advice) require real practitioners; 2027 subscription rules.
- Solo-founder feasibility: hard across many domains (JustAnswer needed about 12K experts). Feasible in one or two domains.

**Interpretation for the founder**
- **Frequency winners are not the money winners.** Tech help for seniors and renewal prompting create monthly touchpoints. Mortgage and health create the revenue. The strongest combined design is probably a **household "call me" line** with (a) a family-paid tech-help subscription as the frequency engine, and (b) proactive renewal prompts plus house-hunt support as the monetisation, routed to one licensed partner per financial vertical. On the money side, the founder acts as a credit representative or authorised representative, or an introducer under the partner's licence, not as a lead seller.
- **The "no-spam, masked, consented" architecture is a regulatory asset** in 2026–27. It matches the ACCC's opt-in recommendation and is the opposite of the lead-auction conduct ASIC alleged against Clark Family. It should be the core brand promise, and the founder should never resell or share contact data beyond the provider the user chose.
- **Avoid the JustAnswer playbook in Australia.** Teaser-to-subscription conversion is the subject of an FTC suit, and from 1 July 2027 it falls under the ACL unfair-trading and subscription rules. Price transparently: per call, or a family plan with clear renewal consent.
- **Biggest unknowns to test cheaply:** (1) whether adult children will pay a monthly fee for "someone calls Mum"; (2) whether a partner broker will take a credit-representative or introducer arrangement and what they will pay per qualified callback; (3) whether proactive renewal texts actually move the realised shopping rate above the roughly 20% baseline.

### Gaps
- All scores are judgement-based. There is no primary demand data (surveys, search volumes, conversion rates) for any candidate in the specific text-a-number format.
- No per-referral fee data for general insurance, energy or telco, and no current health commission data, so the lead-value scores for those rows are low-confidence.
- No legal opinion was obtained on whether the founder's specific model fits the credit referrer exemption, would need credit-representative or authorised-representative status, or would be caught by any lead-generation rules that come out of the Treasury consultation. This is the highest-stakes open question for Family 1.
