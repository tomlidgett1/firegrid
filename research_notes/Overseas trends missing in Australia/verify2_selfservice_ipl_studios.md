# Verification 2 (adversarial): Self-service IPL/laser hair-removal studios in Australia and NZ

**VERDICT for Australia: ABSENT. Confidence: 75%.** After 38 searches in English, Chinese (simplified and traditional/Taiwanese wording), Japanese and Korean, I found none of the following in Australia:
- a customer-operated IPL/laser hair-removal studio;
- consumer IPL room hire;
- a consumer IPL device-rental service;
- a franchise of an Asian self-service hair-removal chain.

**New Zealand caveat (new and material).** New Zealand has a working precedent: the **Sea and Stone Spa (free IPL) Club**, run by Gender Minorities Aotearoa with RainbowYOUTH. It is a private, non-commercial club. Transgender members treat their own facial hair with an IPL device after a staff member shows them how. If the brief's NZ-inclusive "counts as similar" test is applied, or the claim is "first in Australia/NZ", the verdict becomes **PARTIAL**. A new entrant could still credibly claim to be the first *commercial* self-service IPL studio *in Australia*.

**Method.** I ran 38 WebSearch calls, all in standard mode, which is the hard cap. I did not use WebFetch. All evidence comes from search-result extracts, so quotes are as given in those extracts.

## Key Question 1: After resolving the leads, is any customer-operated IPL/laser hair-removal studio, room hire or device rental available in Australia or NZ?

### Takeaway
**Australia: none found.** Every lead resolved to one of these:
- staffed clinics;
- home-device retail;
- unstaffed *tanning* studios;
- room rental to practitioners.

**New Zealand: one exception, narrow and non-commercial.** A trans-community IPL club runs at several sites. Members treat themselves after a staff demonstration. I found no commercial self-service IPL studio, IPL room hire or IPL rental in NZ.

#### Lead 6: TGA (ARTG listing) and Tasmania

**TGA: unresolved.** I could not confirm whether salon-grade IPL used only for cosmetic hair removal *must* be on the ARTG.
- In 2019 the TGA consulted on extending the medical-device framework to products "without an intended medical purpose". This mirrors Annex XVI of the EU Medical Device Regulation, which covers IPL and lasers for hair removal.
- P&G and Accord argued that personal-use IPL should be excluded: [P&G submission](https://www.tga.gov.au/sites/default/files/submission-consultation-changes-definitions-scope-md-framework-pga.pdf); [Accord submission](https://www.tga.gov.au/sites/default/files/submission-consultation-changes-definitions-scope-md-framework-accord.pdf).
- The Cosmetic Physicians Society of Australasia urged regulation of IPL and hair-removal lasers: [CPSA submission](https://www.tga.gov.au/sites/default/files/consult-devices-reforms-101130-submission-cps.pdf).
- I did not find the final outcome of the consultation.
- A 2011 MJA InSight commentary said there were no federal rules on laser/IPL hair removal; the TGA regulated such devices only when used for "therapeutic" purposes: [MJA InSight](https://insightplus.mja.com.au/2011/42/jane-mccredie-armed-and-dangerous).
- In practice, some home-use IPL hair-removal units *are* on the ARTG. Examples: Happy Skin Co is listed as a Class 1 medical device; UVN Enterprises has home-use IPL entries, one cancelled at the sponsor's request: [TGA node 306106](https://www.tga.gov.au/node/306106); [TGA node 306105](https://tga.gov.au/node/306105).
- The TGA has cancelled ARTG entries for some hair-removal diode lasers, for example from Laser Australia Pty Ltd: [TGA](https://www.tga.gov.au/node/185778).
- Electrical safety: under the Electrical Equipment Safety System (EESS), IPLs in Australia must comply with IEC 60335-2-113 from July 2019: [P&G submission](https://www.tga.gov.au/sites/default/files/submission-consultation-changes-definitions-scope-md-framework-pga.pdf).
- A vendor guide says clinics must check a device's ARTG listing and cites penalties above $26,000. This is vendor content and I did not verify it: [MedicalSearch](https://www.medicalsearch.com.au/laser-safety-and-compliance-tga-approved-cosmetic-lasers-and-operator-training/f/26601).

**Tasmania: resolved.**
- IPL used for cosmetic purposes is a regulated radiation source under the *Radiation Protection Act 2005*.
- You need a licence to "deal with" a radiation source, which includes using and possessing it. Dealing with one other than under a licence is an offence under s13: [Tas Dept of Health](https://www.health.tas.gov.au/health-topics/radiation-protection/what-radiation-protection).
- The Department publishes a list of people accredited to test and certify IPL and cosmetic-laser premises and equipment (March 2024): [Tas Health PDF](https://www.health.tas.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-03/ipl_and_cosmetic_accredited_people.pdf).
- A 2012 training document describes accreditation of the equipment, the premises *and the operator*: [training.gov.au](https://training.gov.au/TrainingComponentFiles/SIB10/SIBBHRS706A_R2.pdf).

**Other states**
- According to ARPANSA (the federal radiation safety agency), only Tasmania, Queensland and WA have regulatory controls; Victoria and NSW have none: [Cosmos](https://cosmosmagazine.com/health/safety-agency-evidence-on-cosmetic-laser).
- ARPANSA has flagged burns, scarring and eye damage. It said there is no national approach to cosmetic devices of this kind beyond the solarium ban: [PS News, 26 Sep 2023](https://psnews.com.au/?p=81901).
- A 2021 Queensland parliamentary answer noted that cosmetic IPL was not regulated, unlike cosmetic lasers: [Qld Parliament](https://documents.parliament.qld.gov.au/tableoffice/questionsanswers/2021/80-2021.pdf).
- RACGP (2017): non-medical practitioners need a licence to use lasers in Queensland and Tasmania; in WA, lasers are restricted to registered medical practitioners. Tasmania also licenses IPL, and most other jurisdictions did not require licensing for IPL: [RACGP AFP](https://www.racgp.org.au/afp/2017/september/navigating-the-disparate-australian-regulatory-minefield-of-cosmetic-therapy/).

### Inferences
- **Australia: ABSENT.** This independently agrees with verifier 1's result for Australia. The 38 searches covered:
  - four languages;
  - Box Hill/Chatswood directly, and other target suburbs only through city-level queries;
  - booking platforms;
  - rental and peer-to-peer platforms;
  - unstaffed-studio operators.

  They produced no customer-operated IPL/laser offering, no consumer IPL room hire, no consumer IPL rental and no franchise of an Asian self-service chain.
- **Australia and NZ together: PARTIAL.** The NZ club shows that "staff demonstrates, member then self-treats" IPL already operates in the region. Its format is free, members-only, trans-specific and facial-only.
  - A new Australian entrant can claim to be the "first commercial self-service IPL studio in Australia".
  - It should not claim to be "the first place in Australia/NZ where customers operate IPL themselves".
- **Design precedent.** The NZ club combines a private-club structure, membership and a staff induction. This is a ready model for an Australian membership design. I did not find the legal reason for the club structure.
- **Regulatory map** (inferred from the cited sources):
  - **Tasmania** looks impractical: anyone who uses a cosmetic IPL appears to need a licence, so each customer would need one.
  - **NSW and Victoria** have no IPL-specific operator rules, which makes them the most permissive launch markets.
  - **Queensland** regulated cosmetic lasers but not IPL as of 2021, so an IPL-only model may be possible there. Current law needs confirming.
  - **WA** restricts lasers to registered medical practitioners, so laser-based self-service looks unlikely there. WA's position on IPL is unconfirmed.
- **Insurance is likely the binding constraint.** The UK benchmark wording ties cover to a qualified operator, so self-operation by customers would probably need bespoke cover. I saw no Australian policy wording on this point.
- **Japanese chains are not near-term entrants.** ONESELF, King's Salon and Heidi show no overseas activity, and commentary describes franchise distress in the Japanese self-service sector. Neither suggests these chains will enter Australia soon.

### Gaps
- **Social platforms and maps.** WebSearch cannot query Instagram, TikTok, Xiaohongshu, WeChat, Facebook groups or Google Maps directly. A small community-language operator advertising only on Xiaohongshu or WeChat could exist undetected. This is the main reason confidence is 75% rather than higher.
- **Target suburbs.** Budget limits meant I did not run separate searches for Eastwood, Strathfield, Hurstville, Burwood, Melbourne CBD, Sunnybank or Southport. These suburbs were covered only through city-level queries in each language.
- **Booking platforms.** Bookwell and Square listings never surfaced. I could not search inside Fresha or Timely.
- **Peer-to-peer rental.** Gumtree, Facebook Marketplace and Open Shed listings for IPL hire are not indexed by the search tool.
- **TGA.** Still unconfirmed: whether cosmetic-only salon IPL must be on the ARTG, and the outcome of the 2019 consultation on products without a medical purpose.
- **Tasmania and WA.** I did not find Tasmania's current licence categories and fees, or whether customers could hold a user licence. WA's position on IPL is unknown: a 2022 Radiological Council document surfaced, but its content was not visible.
- **Insurers.** I found no position from Guild, Aon, Gallagher, BizCover or "BIBA" on devices operated by customers.
- **New Zealand.** I did not search NZ's regulatory position on IPL use. For the Sea and Stone club, I did not verify:
  - whether the device is home-use or salon-grade;
  - whether it is operating as of October 2026;
  - whether sessions are in a private room or supervised.

## Key Question 2: What is the closest thing found, and how does it differ?

### Takeaway
**Closest overall:** the NZ Sea and Stone Spa (free IPL) Club, where members treat themselves with IPL after a staff demonstration. It differs from the concept in these ways:
- it is non-commercial and free;
- it is limited to trans people living in NZ;
- it treats facial hair only;
- it operates at staffed sites rather than through app booking and smart locks.

**Closest in Australia:**
- *Same format, different service:* unstaffed, app/Bluetooth-access 24/7 tanning studios (La Dorada, Tanning Bare, Tan Lounge, Coast Tanning).
- *Same service, different format:* staffed IPL clinics and home IPL devices.

### Inferences
- **Format.** The closest format in Australia (unstaffed, app-booked, smart-lock private rooms) already exists and works for tanning, as La Dorada, Tanning Bare, Coast Tanning and Tan Lounge show. A new entrant could reuse the same access technology or partner with these operators.
- **Why no one has added IPL.** None of these operators has added IPL. This points to regulation and insurance as the obstacles rather than technology.
- **Service.** The NZ trans IPL club shows customers can be inducted to treat themselves well enough to keep a multi-site programme running since about 2018. I saw no incident data, so this is an inference.
- **Tan Lounge's razor vending** is the only self-service hair-removal element I found in an Australian unstaffed studio. It is minor (shaving), but it shows unstaffed tanning operators already sell hair-removal prep products.

### Gaps
- I found no incident or safety data for the NZ club, and no detail on its device or level of supervision.
- I could not establish whether La Dorada, Tanning Bare, Tan Lounge or Coast Tanning have considered adding IPL; that would need operator interviews.
- I could not confirm Coast Tanning's location or Tan Lounge's opening date.
