# Exoskeleton hire at hiking trails and tourist sites: second adversarial verification (Australia / New Zealand)

Scope: the second, independent check of whether anyone in Australia or NZ hires out (or lends) powered hip/leg exoskeletons such as Hypershell, Dnsys or Kenqing to hikers, walkers or tourists. I deliberately did not read the first verifier's notes. Date of check: 13 October 2026. 38 WebSearch calls (all "standard" mode, at the hard cap; the full log is at the end). WebFetch was not used, so every finding below comes from search-result extracts. Single-page details (prices, dates) should be read with that limit in mind.

## Key Question 1: After resolving the leads, is any exoskeleton hire for leisure walking/hiking/tourism available in Australia or NZ?

### Takeaway
**VERDICT: ABSENT. Confidence: 80%.** After working through all seven leads with new angles (Hypershell's own AU site, AU/NZ peer-to-peer and rental marketplaces, Airbnb Experiences, named tour operators, Chinese-language queries, parks, NZ, and regulators), I found no paid or free exoskeleton hire or loan for hikers, walkers or tourists anywhere in Australia or New Zealand. I also found no pop-up, pilot or announcement. The closest thing is Hypershell's own **Host Program**, which its Australian storefront promotes. It offers free, owner-led try-outs, but I found no evidence of any Australian host or meetup. It is also a sales funnel, not hire. If active AU hosts were confirmed, the verdict would move toward PARTIAL at most.

**Lead 7: regulation (resolved for Vic, Qld, Tas and TGA principles)**
- **Victoria:**
  - A tour operator and activity provider licence is required to conduct "an organised tour or recreational activity, including training, for profit on public land", and not-for-profit status is no exemption.
  - A one-off activity likely needs an event permit instead.
  - Third parties delivering licensed activities must also hold a licence.
  - Parks Victoria administers licensing for DEECA, and licences can run up to 10 years.
  - ABLIS lists $20m public liability, first-aid-qualified guides and an ABN as requirements.
  - DEECA is reviewing licensing policy and fees; a "TOL Policy 2026" is referenced.
  - Sources: [Parks Victoria licensing](https://www.parks.vic.gov.au/get-into-nature/licensed-tour-operators/licensing); [Forests & Reserves Vic](https://forestsandreserves.vic.gov.au/tour-operators); [ABLIS Vic](https://ablis.business.gov.au/service/vic/tour-operator-licence/24363)
- **Tasmania:**
  - A Nature Based Tourism Licence (Commercial Visitor Services) is needed for tourism-based commercial operations in PWS-managed areas, covering guided tours, adventure activities and cruises. — [ABLIS Tas](https://ablis.business.gov.au/service/tas/nature-based-tourism-licence/7374); [PWS conducting business](https://parks.tas.gov.au/about-us/conducting-business-in-parks-and-reserves)
  - 275 operators were licensed at 31 July 2019. — [NRE Tas RTI](https://nre.tas.gov.au/Documents/RTI%20005%20-2019-20.pdf)
  - Wellington Park (kunanyi/Mt Wellington) needs a separate licence: $170 application fee plus $300 + GST a year, usually for 5 years. — [Wellington Park](https://www.wellingtonpark.org.au/commercial-operators)
  - Enforcement against unlicensed tours, for example around Cradle Mountain, is reported to be difficult. — [The Examiner](https://www.examiner.com.au/story/6425682/illegal-tour-bus-monitoring-needs-tidying-up/)
- **Queensland:**
  - A Commercial Activity Permit is generally required where the aim is profit, a paid guide or leader is involved, the activity is ongoing, or fees exceed costs.
  - It requires $20m public liability and workers' compensation insurance, and a free pre-lodgement meeting is available.
  - Sources conflict on the maximum term (3 years, or 5 years proposed).
  - Sources: [Business Queensland CAP requirements](https://www.business.qld.gov.au/industries/hospitality-tourism-sport/tourism/parks-recreation-forests/commercial-activity-permit/requirements); [QPWS permits review](https://parks.des.qld.gov.au/management/reviewing-queenslands-protected-areas/permits-activities); [ABLIS Qld](https://ablis.business.gov.au/service/qld/commercial-activity-permit/4365)
- **TGA:**
  - Whether a product is a medical device turns on the intended purpose the manufacturer sets, judged from labelling, instructions for use, advertising and technical documentation.
  - Products with similar features can differ in status because of their claims; for example, a mouthguard advertised for contact sports is excluded.
  - Sources: [TGA regulatory basics (PDF)](https://www.tga.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-04/regulatory-basics-medical-devices-health-practitioners.pdf); [TGA "Is my product a medical device?"](https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/decision-trees/my-product-medical-device); [TGA boundary & combination products guidance](https://www.tga.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-12/guidance-boundary-combination-products.pdf)
- Medical exoskeletons on the ARTG show the line between medical and consumer use:
  - Wandercraft Atalante X was listed as a Class IIa device in February 2026, for rehabilitation. — [Wandercraft](https://www.wandercraft.eu/articles/wandercraft-announces-availability-in-australia)
  - ReWalk has TGA approval and an Australian distributor. — [Lifeward/ReWalk](https://golifeward.com/blog/rewalk-robotics-announces-australian-distribution-agreement-tga-approval/)
  - Rex Bionics' home-use ambulation exoskeleton entry was cancelled. — [TGA notice](https://tga.gov.au/node/427045)
- I found no TGA statement specific to consumer hip exoskeletons such as Hypershell (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- With nothing found across two independent passes (38 + 38 searches, English and Chinese), there is very likely no ongoing exoskeleton hire for tourists or hikers in AU or NZ as of 13 October 2026. A new entrant could credibly claim to be first with a staffed hire service at trailheads or attractions.
- The main pre-emption risk is **Hypershell itself**:
  - It runs an AU storefront with local delivery and 14-day returns, put its launch release on the AAP wire, and already runs free try-out pop-ups (Seoul), hiking try-out events (Japan) and destination rental partnerships (Achensee, Austria; see KQ2).
  - It could switch on AU host meetups or pop-ups at short notice. That would cover the free "try it on a trail" use case, though not paid on-the-day hire.
- Buying and returning within 14 days is a do-it-yourself substitute some consumers could use for a single trip. It is not a service, and Hypershell may police it.
- Licensing implications:
  - A trailhead kiosk inside a national park would almost certainly need a commercial licence or permit (Vic tour operator licence, Qld CAP, Tas NBT licence) or a lease or concession.
  - The Victorian and Queensland wording centres on guided or organised activities, so the route for unguided equipment hire inside parks is unclear.
  - Hiring from a town base outside the park, or partnering with private attractions such as Scenic World or with licensed tour operators, avoids much of this.
- TGA implications:
  - Marketing the devices as leisure and hiking performance aids is likely to keep them outside the medical-device rules.
  - Marketing them to people with medical conditions (arthritis, post-stroke, "mobility impairment") could bring them inside.
  - This follows from the intended-purpose test and has not been confirmed by a TGA ruling.

## Key Question 2: What is the closest thing found, and how does it differ?

### Takeaway
The closest thing is Hypershell's **Host Program**, advertised on its Australian site: free, owner-led try-outs. It is not hire, it is a pre-purchase sales funnel, and I found no evidence of any AU host or meetup. Everything else in AU/NZ is materially different:
- direct sales with returns;
- industrial exosuit rent-to-buy (SpanSet/Auxivo);
- rehabilitation exoskeletons on the ARTG;
- all-terrain wheelchairs in parks;
- porterage and accessible tours;
- ordinary hiking-gear hire.

### Inferences
- The overseas pattern points to brand-led rental at destinations (Achensee, Seoul pop-up, Japan event) and small tour operators building walks around the devices (Budapest). Both could be copied in AU quickly. Hypershell is better placed than anyone to launch AU try-outs. The defensible part for a new entrant is the paid, staffed, on-site hire model (fitting, safety briefing, battery swaps) under park or attraction partnerships, not simply "first to show the device".
- Several AU findings point to unmet demand among older and less-fit visitors: Intrepid's porterage marketed to people limited by "injury or age", Ayers Rock Resort's wheelchairs that cannot enter the park, and Grey Nomads coverage pitched at older caravanners. This supports the concept's target segment but does not prove demand.
