# Independent verification: AI-powered robotic massage (robot-arm massage bed, app-booked, placed in gyms/hotels/airports/studios) — does it exist in Australia?

**VERDICT: ABSENT** — **Confidence: 75%** (as at 13 October 2026)

No Australian business, distributor, gym, hotel, airport, clinic, studio or start-up offering robotic-arm/AI massage was found, and no Australian pilot, trial or launch announcement was found. 36 web searches ran across overseas entrants, Australian cities, Australian media, trade press, social and community sources. Everything found in Australia is materially different: massage chairs, massage guns, therapist-operated aesthetic machines, rehab robots, and a 2019 Swinburne cobot laser-therapy prototype. Confidence is capped at 75% for three reasons: mainstream Australian news domains (AFR, SMH, The Age, news.com.au) and Reddit could not be searched; Chinese-language channels were not searched; and cheap Chinese massage cobots are openly sold for export, so a quiet import by an individual clinic cannot be ruled out.

**Critical context for the parent team (not part of the absence test):** the lead overseas exemplar, **Aescape, entered insolvency in May 2026 with over US$150m of debt** after a foreclosure sale of substantially all its assets earlier in 2026 ([BeautyMatter](https://beautymatter.com/articles/robot-massage-company-aescape-entered-insolvency-with-150-m-in-debt)). Australian trade press carried a headline about "fears for wellness tech investments following collapse of Aescape robotic massage venture" (ausleisure.com.au; see the Q1 findings). The claim that the concept is "proven overseas" is therefore weakened. The Chinese players (Yiqi Future) and the French player (Capsix) remain active.

## Q1. Can Australian consumers book a robotic/AI massage anywhere in Australia today, or has any pilot run?

### Takeaway
No. Every overseas robotic-massage player checked (Aescape, Capsix iYU, Massage Robotics "Alex", RoboSculptor, AiTreat EMMA, Yiqi Future/"Yee Future", Third Space "Self", Flexiv, AIBotics "Phill", Son-ok R2E) shows no Australian or New Zealand location, distributor, pilot or announcement. City-level searches (Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Gold Coast, Perth, Adelaide, Canberra, Auckland), plus searches on gym chains, recovery studios, hotels, airport lounges, Westfield, and physio and aesthetic clinics, returned only human massage, massage chairs or massage guns.

### Inferences
- Robotic massage is confined to North America, Europe, Singapore, China and Korea. Distribution deals and stated target markets (Capsix: Europe and North America; Earthlite: North America, Mexico and Caribbean) exclude Australia, and Aescape never went beyond North America before its insolvency.
- A credible Australian launch or pilot would very likely have produced "first in Australia" coverage in Time Out, Broadsheet, Startup Daily or the trade press, and none appeared. ## Q2. What is the closest thing found, and how does it differ?

### Takeaway
The closest Australian items are:
- a **2019 Swinburne University cobot prototype** that automatically finds pain "hot spots" with a thermal camera and treats them with a robot-positioned laser. It is a robot treating soft tissue, but it is laser photobiomodulation rather than massage, and it never became a commercial service;
- **massage chairs and massage guns in gym recovery zones**, an excluded category;
- **therapist-operated mechanical massage machines** in aesthetic studios (Endosphères, Bodi Slim G5);
- an **ARTG-registered Ceragem automated thermal massage bed**. Its Australian retail presence was not verified, and it is a roller mechanism, not a robot arm.

None of these delivers an autonomous, 3D-scanned, robot-arm full-body massage booked through an app.

### Inferences
- No Australian offering does "essentially the same job": an autonomous, personalised, full-body massage with no therapist, booked by app and placed in venues. A new entrant could plausibly claim to be first in Australia, subject to the unresolved leads below.
- The Swinburne prototype is the only Australian robot-arm soft-tissue therapy project found. It is laser-based, dates from 2019 and was never commercialised. It is evidence of local cobot and therapy know-how (Swinburne and IR Robotics), not of prior market existence.

## Q3. What Australian facts on demand or regulation matter?

### Takeaway
The adjacent Australian spa and wellness market is large but flat (about A$2.8bn, roughly 1% a year). The massage workforce is modest, part-time and low-paid, with projected growth of only about 1% a year in Victoria. Gyms are adding recovery zones. On regulation, a robot marketed with therapeutic claims (pain relief) would probably need ARTG inclusion via an Australian sponsor, since massagers already sit on the ARTG as Class I and Class IIa medical devices.

### Cited Findings (5 facts)
1. **Market size.** IBISWorld puts Australia's Health & Wellness Spas market at about A$2.8bn in 2026: down 0.6% that year, with 1.0% compound annual growth over 2021–2026. Massage is one component, not the whole figure; no dedicated IBISWorld massage report was found ([IBISWorld](https://www.ibisworld.com/au/market-size/health-wellness-spas/)).
2. **Workforce supply (Victoria).** About 4,473 massage therapists worked in Victoria in 2023. Workforce growth is projected at 1.0% for 2023–2026, with 354 new workers needed by 2026. The national median weekly wage is A$1,472 (Jobs and Skills Australia data, June 2024) ([Victorian Government](https://www.vic.gov.au/massage-therapist)).
3. **Workforce structure.** In a national survey of 301 AMT members, 73.8% worked 20 hours a week or less and only 5.7% earned above the average wage from massage. This is a largely part-time, low-earning workforce, which limits supply at peak times. The survey is from 2015 ([UTS OPUS](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/37259)).
4. **Regulation (TGA).** The AMT notes that the TGA's remit covers devices making therapeutic claims, naming percussion tools among examples that may be captured ([AMT fact sheet](https://www.amt.org.au/downloads/practice-resources/AMT-Fact-Sheet-Use-of-therapeutic-devices-and-massage-tools.pdf)). ARTG search results show massagers registered as medical devices, including a Class I "Massager, <specify>" and Class IIa physical-therapy massagers, one of them Ceragem's ([TGA](https://www.tga.gov.au/node/294712); [TGA](https://www.tga.gov.au/node/243121); [TGA](https://tga.gov.au/node/262061)).
5. **Recovery demand inside gyms.** Fitness First Australia launched its "Revive" recovery zone (infrared saunas, cold therapy, massage guns, compression boots) at its Melbourne flagship in Sept 2024 and planned to roll out popular elements across its clubs ([Fitt Insider](https://insider.fitt.co/fitness-first-australia-debuts-revive-recovery-concept/)).

### Inferences
- The demand case in Australia rests on convenience, off-hours availability and price rather than an acute national therapist shortage. Workforce data shows slow growth and part-time supply, but no official "shortage" finding was confirmed for 2026.
- How the product is positioned drives regulatory cost. "Relaxation and wellness" claims may avoid ARTG inclusion. Pain or injury treatment claims would likely require an Australian sponsor and ARTG entry (Class I or IIa, by analogy with existing massagers). This is an inference, not a TGA ruling.
