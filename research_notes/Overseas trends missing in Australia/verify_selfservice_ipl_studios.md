# Independent verification: Self-service (customer-operated) IPL hair-removal studios in Australia, and whether regulation allows them

Prepared 13 October 2026 by an independent, adversarial verifier with no stake in the idea passing. Evidence base: 36 WebSearch calls in "standard" mode (21 on presence, 15 on regulation, overseas growth and demand). WebFetch was unavailable, so every finding below comes from search-result extracts, not full-page reads. Page-level details (dates, numbers, quotes) should be read as extract-level evidence, and the stated caveats should be kept.

---

## Q1. Is any self-service (customer-operated) IPL or laser hair-removal studio operating, piloted or announced in Australia?

### Takeaway
**VERDICT: ABSENT. Confidence: 80%.** 21 presence searches were run in English, Japanese, Korean and Chinese. They covered Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Perth, Adelaide, Canberra and the Gold Coast, plus rental, room-hire, unstaffed/24-7 and "first launch" angles. None found an Australian studio where customers operate an IPL or laser themselves, whether staffed or not. None found a consumer IPL-rental service, or an Australian franchise or expansion of a Japanese, Korean or Chinese self-hair-removal brand. The nearest things are home IPL devices, staffed clinics, mobile at-home technicians, and unstaffed 24/7 app-access spray-tan studios. Each is materially different (see Q2).

### Inferences
- A customer-operated IPL studio would be a novelty. It would likely show up in local news, booking directories or business-for-sale listings. None of the 21 presence searches surfaced one, so an operating, publicly marketed studio is unlikely.
- Two blind spots keep confidence at 80% rather than higher. First, a small operator serving a migrant community (Chinese 自助脱毛 or Japanese セルフ脱毛) that markets only on Xiaohongshu, WeChat, LINE or Instagram, none of which web search indexes well. Second, informal "use our IPL room yourself" arrangements inside existing salons. Neither can be checked without Maps or social-media access, or phone calls.
- On current evidence, a new entrant could plausibly claim to be "Australia's first self-service IPL hair-removal studio". Before using that claim publicly, it should run a Google Maps, Instagram and Xiaohongshu sweep.

## Q2. What is the closest thing found in Australia, and how does each differ?

### Takeaway
Nothing found does the whole job: a customer operating a salon-grade IPL in a private, bookable studio at a fraction of clinic prices. The closest functional substitutes are **home IPL devices** (Happy Skin Co, Philips Lumea, Beurer on sale at JB Hi-Fi). The customer operates these, but they must be bought and are consumer-grade. The closest *format* precedents are **unstaffed, app-access 24/7 spray-tan studios** (La Dorada in Shepparton; an automated spray-tan studio for sale in Brisbane). These prove smart-lock, unstaffed beauty rooms already exist in Australia, but not for IPL. All other findings are staffed clinics, mobile technicians, or business-to-business room or equipment arrangements.

### Inferences
- Home IPL devices are the strongest substitute for the customer's job (cheap, private, self-done IPL). Several things set the studio concept apart and stop it being "present": no upfront purchase of A$hundreds, higher-power salon machines, guided onboarding, and a pay-per-session or membership model. In Japan, ONESELF grew to about 90 stores despite cheap home IPLs being widely sold there. That suggests the studio and home-device markets can coexist (inference only; no Japanese market-share data was gathered).
- The unstaffed spray-tan studios (#8, #9) are useful for operations and the investor story. Smart locks, app access and CCTV-monitored unstaffed beauty rooms are already accepted by Australian consumers and landlords.
- No consumer IPL rental, and no IPL room hire to consumers, was found. Under the brief's rules this would have counted as PARTIAL, so its absence supports the ABSENT verdict.

### Gaps
- Several entities have no dates in the extracts (#8–#12).
- Self-service teeth-whitening studios in Australia were not searched (budget). They are low relevance to the verdict.

## Q3. In which states is a self-service IPL studio legally workable, and what licences or design changes would be required?

### Takeaway
Australia has **no national licensing framework** for cosmetic lasers or IPL. **Only Tasmania regulates IPL**, while **Queensland, Tasmania and Western Australia regulate cosmetic lasers** ([ARPANSA](https://arpansa.gov.au/understanding-radiation/sources-radiation/more-radiation-sources/lasers-and-intense-pulsed-light-ipl)). So under radiation-safety law, an **IPL-only** self-service studio appears workable in NSW, Victoria, Queensland, WA, SA, the ACT and the NT. It is **not workable in Tasmania** without an exemption that may not exist. A **laser-based** (e.g. diode) self-service studio is not workable in Qld, WA or Tas. The real constraints elsewhere are likely to be insurance, Australian Consumer Law liability and the risk of regulators reacting. Injury reports blame operator error in 93% of the cases that state a cause, and in this model the customer is the operator.

### Cited Findings: national (ARPANSA, TGA), injuries and insurance
- ARPANSA says "lasers for cosmetic purposes are only regulated in Queensland, Tasmania and Western Australia, while IPLs are only regulated in Tasmania". Jurisdictions without a framework do not require a licence for light-based cosmetic treatments — [ARPANSA lasers & IPL page](https://arpansa.gov.au/understanding-radiation/sources-radiation/more-radiation-sources/lasers-and-intense-pulsed-light-ipl); [ARPANSA advice for providers](https://www.arpansa.gov.au/advice-providers-lasers-ipl-devices-and-led-phototherapy-cosmetic-treatments-and-beauty-therapy)
- In 2023 ARPANSA said there is "no national approach to regulation of devices or services that use non-ionising radiation for cosmetic purposes, with the exception of the ban on solariums" — [ARPANSA news 2023](https://www.arpansa.gov.au/news/arpansa-reviews-risks-cosmetic-procedures-0); [PS News, 26 Sep 2023](https://psnews.com.au/?p=81901)
- ARPANSA's 2015 national consultation (Regulation Impact Statement) found "insufficient information… to support the introduction of a national regulatory framework". It moved instead to advice focused on consumer education and provider competency (advisory note, August 2018) — [ARPANSA consultation RIS](https://www.arpansa.gov.au/sites/default/files/legacy/pubs/comment/IPL_ConsultationRIS.pdf); [ARPANSA analysis of submissions TR177](https://www.arpansa.gov.au/sites/default/files/tr177.pdf); [ARPANSA advisory note 2018](https://www.arpansa.gov.au/sites/default/files/advisory_for_light-based_procedures_in_the_cosmetic_and_beauty_therapy_industry_final1.pdf); [NSW EPA](https://epa.nsw.gov.au/Your-environment/Radiation/protection-information/info-lasers)
- Per the search extract, ARPANSA is "looking into the risks" of cosmetic laser and IPL treatments, which "could lead to national industry regulation". A 2022 ARPANSA review called for uniform operator training requirements. The article's date was not visible — [Cosmos](https://cosmosmagazine.com/health/safety-agency-evidence-on-cosmetic-laser)
- Injuries: a 2012 ARPANSA survey counted 416 injuries in 735,920 procedures over the previous 12 months (attribution to the RIS inferred from the extract). ARPANSA's media-report study "Does beauty burn?" found 95 unique injuries (laser 60, IPL 29). Where a cause was described, 93% involved operator-related factors — [ARPANSA consultation RIS](https://www.arpansa.gov.au/sites/default/files/legacy/pubs/comment/IPL_ConsultationRIS.pdf); [ARPANSA "Does beauty burn?"](https://www.arpansa.gov.au/does-beauty-burn-study-media-reports-burns-cosmetic-nir-devices-australia)
- TGA: the register (ARTG) includes a Happy Skin Co IPL device for cosmetic skin treatment, including hair removal (Class I medical device, 2019). It also lists a Venus Concept hair-removal diode laser, and a cancelled Laser Australia hair-removal diode laser (s41GL(d)). In 2019 Procter & Gamble asked TGA that personal-use IPLs "be exempted from this scope, and not be regulated as medical device"; the outcome was not found. A vendor guide claims IPLs are Class IIa/IIb, but this is unverified — [TGA ARTG node 306105](https://tga.gov.au/node/306105); [TGA ARTG 308028](https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/artg/308028); [TGA cancellation](https://www.tga.gov.au/node/185778); [P&G submission](https://www.tga.gov.au/sites/default/files/submission-consultation-changes-definitions-scope-md-framework-pga.pdf); [medicalsearch (vendor)](https://www.medicalsearch.com.au/avoiding-costly-mistakes-how-to-verify-tga-approval-before-purchasing-an-ipl-machine/f/26620)
- Overseas regulator view: France's ANSES recommends that IPL hair-removal devices be covered by a suitable regulatory framework because of burn and eye-damage risks — [ANSES](https://www.anses.fr/en/node/150946)
- Insurance: no Australian insurer's criteria were found. UK benchmark: the BABTAC laser/IPL extension requires Level 2 and 3 beauty plus a Level 3 electrical qualification (or doctor or nurse) and a certificate of competence for the operator. It excludes clients who are pregnant, under 18, on photosensitising medication, or have light sensitivity or skin cancer. A UK broker says laser cover generally needs Level 4 training — [BABTAC endorsement](https://WWW.BABTAC.COM/userfiles/files/BABTAC%20Laser%20and%20Light%20Therapies%20including%20IPL%20up%20to%20skin%20type%20VI%20extension%20Endorsement.pdf); [Ray Cochrane](https://www.raycochrane.co.uk/insurance-for-laser-and-ipl-treatments/)
- Australian training norms: TAFE NSW's SHB60118 Advanced Diploma of IPL and Laser for Hair Reduction requires 12 months' post-qualification beauty-therapy experience. Beauty therapists are expected to carry professional and public liability insurance — [TAFE NSW SHB60118](https://tafensw.edu.au/course-areas/hair-/courses/advanced-diploma-of-intense-pulsed-light-and-laser-for-hair-reduction--SHB60118-01); [Pathway to Australia FAQ](https://pathwaytoaus.com/faq/how-do-i-become-a-beauty-therapist-in-australia/)

### Inferences
- **Where it is workable (radiation law only):** IPL-only studios in NSW, Victoria, Queensland, WA, SA, the ACT and the NT. Tasmania should be excluded at launch. Diode or other lasers should be avoided in Qld, WA and Tas, where every user needs a licence or registration and, in WA, medical-practitioner protocols.
- **The real constraints are insurance and liability, not licensing.** Injury reports blame operator factors in 93% of cases with a stated cause, and insurers benchmark cover on operator competence (BABTAC). A pure "customer is the operator" model will be hard to insure and attractive to regulators. Australian Consumer Law consumer guarantees cannot be contracted out of. This point is analyst knowledge and was not researched here.
- **Likely design changes needed:** (a) IPL only, using devices on the ARTG; (b) energy settings locked in software by Fitzpatrick skin type, with a skin-tone sensor or interlock; (c) a mandatory in-app tutorial plus a first-visit skin assessment, in person or by video, by a qualified therapist; (d) BABTAC-style exclusion screening (pregnancy, under 18, photosensitising drugs, skin cancer); (e) eye protection and interlocks, CCTV or remote monitoring, and an on-call therapist; (f) a "minimally staffed" variant (one trained supervisor covering several rooms) to make insurance easier; (g) watch ARPANSA and state reform. The Queensland and WA IPL carve-outs are explicitly "currently" in force, and a visible self-service model could prompt rule changes.
- Using consumer-grade (home) IPL devices in the studio would lower risk and regulatory exposure. It would also weaken the "salon-grade" value proposition, and Tasmania's position would still be unclear.

### Gaps
- No primary current statements for SA, the ACT or the NT; no NSW 2025–26 primary source; ARPANSA's "regulated only in…" page is undated.
- Whether Tasmania's licensing captures low-power devices; whether any state would treat a customer-operated device as the business "using" it.
- TGA's position on ARTG inclusion for salon IPLs used only for cosmetic hair removal.
- Australian insurer appetite; council registration rules; WHS duties.

## Q4. What do the overseas exemplars show (dated growth figures)?

### Takeaway
ONESELF is confirmed as a fast-growing, franchise-led, unmanned self-hair-removal chain in Japan. **The three strongest dated figures** are: 86 stores and 76,000 members as of 31 January 2026; the 90th store (Maebashi) opening on 26 September 2026; and the 82nd store (Takaoka) opening on 7 June 2025. **Adversarial note:** the pace has slowed sharply, from about 80 stores in the first four years to about 8 stores in the 15 months from June 2025 to September 2026. The wider Japanese hair-removal salon sector has also seen bankruptcies. No Korean or Chinese self-IPL chain data was found.

### Cited Findings
1. **ONESELF: 86 stores and 76,000 members as of 31 January 2026** (現在・全国86店舗・会員数76,000人, base date 2026年1月31日) — [san-tatsu press release](https://san-tatsu.jp/pressrelease/556225/)

On 7 November 2022 an unmanned self hair-removal salon opened in Fukuyama, Hiroshima, priced "20 minutes from ¥1,000" (brand probably ONESELF; not confirmed in the extract) — [BCN Retail](https://www.bcnretail.com/news/detail/20221107_303451.html)

### Inferences
- The format has clearly scaled in Japan (about 90 stores in about 5 years, under one brand). But the slowdown from about 20 net openings a year (2021 to mid-2025) to about 6 a year (mid-2025 to Sept 2026) suggests saturation or franchise-supply limits. Note that "90th store" counts openings cumulatively, so the net operating count may be lower if stores have closed.
- Japan's pricing (about ¥1,000 per 20 minutes in 2022) works out at roughly A$10 per session (analyst conversion, not sourced). That is close to Australian clinics' promotional small-area prices (from A$10–$29; see Q5). The Australian price advantage would therefore come mainly from large areas (legs, back) and unlimited memberships, not small areas.

### Gaps
- Store counts for other Japanese self-service chains (e.g. ハイジ/Heidi), and evidence of Korean self-IPL chains (Korean medical law may restrict IPL to doctors; not verified here) or Chinese 自助脱毛 chains: none found.
- The proposal's "85th store, December 2025" was not directly confirmed (consistent with 82 in June 2025 and 86 on 31 January 2026).
- Japan's legal basis for customer-operated IPL salons was not researched.

## Q5. What are the Australian demand facts (market size, prices, therapist shortage)?

### Takeaway
Australia's hair-removal market is about **US$950.5 million (2024)**. Analysts flag rising demand for at-home IPL and laser devices, which is evidence of a "do it yourself, cheaply" demand segment. Clinic IPL prices are widely spread: **promotional small areas from about A$10–$29, larger areas A$90–$200 per session, over 6–10 sessions.** Evidence on a formal beauty-therapist shortage is conflicting.

### Inferences
- Demand for cheap, self-directed hair removal is real: the home-device trend, a large market, and cost complaints. A studio could also capture consumers who reject the upfront cost or lower power of home devices.
- Australian clinics already discount small areas heavily. The studio's price story should therefore centre on large-area, unlimited-membership use (full legs, back, full body), where clinic prices of A$90–$200 per session over 6–10 sessions add up.
- If a beauty-therapist shortage is confirmed, it strengthens the labour-light case. The evidence is mixed, so it should not be a core part of the pitch until checked against Jobs and Skills Australia data.
