## Which East Asian models grew fastest in 2024–2026 and are NOT on the exclusion list?

### Takeaway
The residual white space is narrow but real. Three new automation formats have dated 2024–26 growth and no Australian equivalent found:
- **Unstaffed self-service hair-removal (IPL) studios** (Japan). ONESELF went from one store in 2021 to 86 stores on 31 Jan 2026.
- **Exoskeleton hire at hiking trails and tourist sites** (China). It went from launch at Qingming 2025 to more than 50 scenic sites by Oct 2026, with 500 units on Mount Tai alone.
- **AI or robotic hair-wash salons** (China, spreading to Hong Kong and Taiwan). Shenzhen had more than 500 stores in 2025.

Two service-heavy models survive as PARTIAL:
- **Specialist home bathing for bedbound older people** (China, Japan, Taiwan).
- **Appliance subscriptions with scheduled care visits** (Korea). LG's subscription revenue passed ₩2tn in 2025.

### Inferences
**Analysis:** concept, revenue model and Australian fit for each survivor.

1. **Self-service hair-removal studios** (strongest fit with the project's past hits).
   - **How it works:** Small, app-booked, unstaffed rooms with salon-grade IPL machines. Customers treat themselves on a subscription or per-session fee. The operator earns on memberships and can franchise, as ONESELF does.
   - **Why it fits Australia:** It removes the therapist wage, the main cost in high-wage Australian clinics. It uses small rooms, which suits high rents. And it undercuts staffed laser pricing; one Melbourne example was A$1,700 for 5 full-body sessions (see Q2).
   - **Risks:**
     - State radiation and IPL rules (Q2).
     - Burns and liability from untrained self-use.
     - Device registration on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG) [Unverified].
     - Substitution by cheap home IPL devices.
     - The bankruptcy churn in Japan's hair-removal salon sector.
2. **Exoskeleton hire at trails and tourist sites.**
   - **How it works:** Hire by the hour or day at trailheads, cable cars, hotels and tour operators, perhaps bundled into guided walks. There is a B2B angle selling fleets-as-a-service to sites. China shows demand among older visitors.
   - **Why it fits Australia:** It needs local operations: device logistics, fitting and charging. It suits an ageing domestic hiker market and walks such as the Blue Mountains, Tasmanian trails, Kosciuszko and Kings Canyon.
   - **Risks:**
     - National-park commercial licences and insurance [Unverified; not searched].
     - Device durability and heat.
     - Customers buying rather than renting, since consumer units are sold in Australia.
     - Seasonality.
3. **AI hair-wash salons.**
   - **How it works:** A low-price wash (and possibly blow-dry) bar in malls or transit hubs, or machines placed into existing salons and barbers. In China, revenue comes from per-wash fees of about ¥19–23 plus machine sales and franchising. Capex is low: one machine costs about ¥20–30k.
   - **Why it fits Australia:** High hairdressing wages favour automating the shampoo step.
   - **Risks:**
     - Quality complaints in China.
     - Possible overlap with the excluded "robotic massage" category, since the machines also massage the scalp.
     - Australian electrical, plumbing and water-safety certification [Unverified].
     - A fad profile.
4. **Specialist home bathing (portable tub at the bedside).**
   - **How it works:** A fee per visit (China ¥398–498; Taiwan's long-term care scheme NT$1,500 per visit), funded in Australia through Support at Home, NDIS or private pay.
   - **Why it fits Australia:** It suits ageing-in-place policy and needs local operations.
   - **Risks:** It is low-tech and labour-heavy; Japan's model uses multi-person teams [Unverified]. Support at Home's single-provider rule (Q2) means operating as a registered provider or as a subcontractor.
5. **Appliance subscriptions with scheduled care visits.**
   - **How it works:** Monthly fees bundle the appliance with periodic technician cleaning, filter changes and servicing. The service network is the moat.
   - **Risks:**
     - Capital-intensive (inventory finance), so likely beyond seed.
     - Incumbents: Radio Rentals and 2ndLease.
     - LG may enter Australia directly (Q2).
     - Consumer-lease regulation [Unverified].
     - Australia's safe tap water weakens the water-purifier anchor product.
- **Analysis: overall pattern.** As in earlier scans, the survivors are *new automation formats from 2025–26* (exoskeleton hire, AI hair-wash). Diaspora entrepreneurs have not copied them yet. Older East Asian formats (sauna rooms, rental studios, luggage delivery, whitening) already have Australian equivalents.

### Inferences
- **Analysis: ranking.** Top 3: (1) self-service hair-removal studios; (2) exoskeleton hire at trails and tourist sites; (3) AI hair-wash salons. Each needs local operations, avoids staff costs or adds tourist value, and can be built with seed money.
  - #1 matches the "unstaffed, app-booked room" pattern that previously screened absent (billiards rooms, robotic massage).
  - #2 has the strongest dated growth: zero to 50+ sites in about 18 months.
  - #3 has the cheapest capex but the weakest product reviews.
- **Analysis: where to launch.** A self-service IPL studio would sensibly launch in NSW or Victoria, which needed no operator licence as of 2017. Avoid Tasmania, and check Queensland and WA first. Whether a customer operating the device counts as an "operator" under state radiation law is untested [Unverified].
- **Analysis: diaspora risk.** It looks low for #2 and #3 today: the Chinese-language searches found nothing. Both formats went viral in China only in 2025–26, so a diaspora copy could appear quickly. First-mover speed matters.
- **Analysis: strict-test caveat.** Robot event hire and private saunas are "PARTIAL at best", not identical to the East Asian formats: Australia has no rental platform for robots and no Sydney or Melbourne unstaffed sauna suites were found. Under the brief's "nothing closely similar" rule they are eliminated.
