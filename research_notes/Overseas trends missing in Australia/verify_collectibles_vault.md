# Verification: vaulted graded-card marketplace with instant buyback and digital pack rips: does it exist in Australia or serve Australians?

This is an independent, adversarial verification, done on 13 October 2026. I ran 37 WebSearch calls in standard mode, under a hard cap of 38. WebFetch was unavailable, so all evidence below comes from search-result extracts, and no page was opened in full. I did not read other research_notes files.

**VERDICT: PRESENT. Confidence: 80%.**

- **Why PRESENT:** Courtyard is the lead overseas exemplar, and it already serves Australian residents.
  - Its Terms exclude only US-embargoed jurisdictions.
  - It ships redeemed cards "to most countries worldwide".
  - Australian reviewers report redeemed cards arriving in Melbourne in under a week.
  - It pays out sale and buyback balances in USDC where bank payouts aren't supported.
- **Other platforms:** Crypto-native equivalents (Collector Crypt, Phygitals, Solflare-hosted packs) run paid random packs with an 85–90% buyback. I found no Australian geo-block for them, though I didn't confirm that Australians can use them.
- **No local operator found:** None of the 15+ Australia-specific queries found an Australian-domiciled operator with a local vault, AUD pricing, or AFL/NRL focus. A new entrant could claim "first Australian-vaulted, AUD-priced" but not "first available to Australians".
- **What would lower confidence:** a primary source showing that Courtyard's card payments and buyback are US-only. One comparison site claims "US residents, 18+", which contradicts Courtyard's own documents and Australian user reviews.

## 1. Does any Australian business, or any overseas platform serving Australians, offer vaulted graded cards with instant trading/buyback or digital pack rips?

### Takeaway
Yes, through overseas platforms.
- Courtyard (US) offers the exact concept and is used by Australians: packs, 90% instant buyback, insured vault, peer-to-peer marketplace, and redemption to Australia.
- Several Solana-based clones probably also accept Australians.
- No Australian business offering vault custody, in-app trading, instant buyback or digital pack rips was found. Australian results were only retail sellers, storage-accessory sellers, grading middlemen and live-break shops.

### Cited Findings

#### Courtyard (US): eligibility for Australian residents
- **Terms of Service.** Use is barred only for people located in, or controlled by, a US-embargoed jurisdiction: Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Syria, and the Crimea, Donetsk and Luhansk regions. Users must be 18+ or able to form a binding contract where they live. No Australian restriction appeared in the extract. — [Courtyard Terms of Service](https://docs.courtyard.io/courtyard/logistics-and-legal/user-agreements/terms-of-service)
- **Redemption.** Courtyard ships to most countries worldwide, with only a few exceptions for legal or logistical reasons. Customers outside the US are responsible for their own import duties. — [Courtyard docs: Asset Redemption & Shipping](https://docs.courtyard.io/courtyard/logistics-and-legal/asset-redemption-and-shipping)
- **Cash-out of $ balance (buyback and sale proceeds).**
  - Bank and debit-card payouts are supported only in specific countries, using third-party provider Coinflow's list.
  - In unsupported countries, users can withdraw USDC to a personal crypto wallet.
  - [Courtyard help: How to Withdraw Your $ Balance](https://help.courtyard.io/en/articles/9201319-how-to-withdraw-your-balance)
- **Australian user reports (Trustpilot AU).**
  - One reviewer: "Shipping is fast, sometimes less than a week to get to Melbourne Aus."
  - Another reviewer calls delivery to Australia speedy.
  - Other reviewers complain that delivery plus customs fees can exceed the card's value.
  - The extract gave a TrustScore of about 3.5/5 from about 777 reviews.
  - [Trustpilot AU: Courtyard.io](https://au.trustpilot.com/review/courtyard.io)
- **Worldwide redemption claim.** Courtyard's Trustpilot profile advertises worldwide redemption. Courtyard told a UK customer it ships "strictly at cost" and does not set UK import taxes. — [Trustpilot: Courtyard.io](https://it.trustpilot.com/review/courtyard.io)
- **Product.** Courtyard offers an instant buyback at 90% of fair market value on pack pulls. Items are graded, insured and vaulted, and are represented by a digital token. — [GoCollect](https://gocollect.com/blog/graded-vaulted-and-virtual)
- **Contradicting source.** A 2026 comparison table lists Courtyard as "From $10 | 90% buyback, minus a 6% fee | US residents, 18+". — [Under30CEO](https://www.under30ceo.com/best-online-card-rip-platforms-in-2026/)
  - Courtyard's own Terms and shipping documents contradict this, as do Australian Trustpilot reviewers.
  - It may reflect card-payment or promotion limits rather than account eligibility, but this is unverified.
- **Scale.**
  - US$30M Series A in July 2025. — [FinTech Global](https://fintech.global/2025/07/29/collectibles-platform-courtyard-io-lands-30m-series-a/)
  - Reported to be "on pace for $2 billion in annual sales", citing Barron's. The source article says it was produced with AI assistance. — [ON360](https://on360.ca/?p=920)
- **Promotions are geo-limited.** The Courtyard "Genesis Raffle", effective 27 March 2026, is void in New York, Florida and any jurisdiction where it would not comply with local law. — [Courtyard help: Genesis Raffle](https://intercom.help/courtyardio/en/articles/14241319-genesis-raffle)

### Inferences
- **Courtyard satisfies the PRESENT rule.** Australians can open accounts. They buy, and redeem to Australia, which reviewers confirm. They very likely can use buyback and marketplace sales, with proceeds withdrawable at least as USDC. Paying by card from Australia is likely but not directly confirmed.
- **Australians already face friction on current platforms:**
  - a US vault
  - USD/USDC pricing
  - international shipping, plus import duty or GST on redemption
  - a US-centric catalogue

  A local entrant would compete on these points (AUD pricing, local vault, AFL/NRL, Japanese Pokémon). That is differentiation, not a first-mover position.
- **The clone set is large and growing.** Some clones ship worldwide (Slabz) or are crypto-native (Collector Crypt, Phygitals), so Australians are probably already served by several of them. Coinbase's teased product could reach Australia at launch.
- **"Absent" would be false.** Treating the concept as absent in Australia would be wrong for end-users. It holds only in the narrow sense of "no Australian-domiciled operator found".

## 2. What is the closest thing found, and how does it differ?

### Takeaway
The closest match is Courtyard. It is the same product, already used by Australians. It differs only in localisation:
- US vault
- USD/USDC pricing
- international shipping plus import duty or GST on redemption
- US-centric inventory, with no evidence of AFL/NRL cards

The next closest are Collector Crypt and Phygitals: the same mechanics on crypto rails, with Australian access unconfirmed. Then come US pack-rip sites, which are the same job but have unknown Australian eligibility. PSA Vault via eBay AU is a vault and consignment with no packs or buyback. No Australian business comes close: "monvault" uses the word "vault" but is an eBay retailer.

## 3. What is the Australian legal risk for the pack-rip and buyback features?

### Takeaway
Paid random packs with a guaranteed instant cash buyback carry a **high risk** of being a "gambling service" under the Interactive Gambling Act 2001 (IGA).
- ACMA's main reason for not treating loot boxes as gambling is the absence of a cash-out. A buyback supplies exactly that.
- ACMA has acted against virtual-item gambling sites and blocks offshore illegal gambling sites.
- Plain vault-and-trade or buyback of specific known cards, with no chance element, carries **low gambling risk**.
- Tokenisation may raise ASIC financial-product or AFSL issues if tokens carry investment-like features, but simple 1:1 title tokens are less exposed. This is not confirmed.
- Overseas, US scrutiny of digital card rips is rising.

### Cited Findings
- **ACMA's position on loot boxes.** In general, online games with loot boxes have "not been regarded as 'gambling services'" under the IGA because they are not "played for money or anything else of value". Features are assessed case by case. — [Senate Environment & Communications Committee report, ch. 2](https://aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Senate/Environment_and_Communications/Gamingmicro-transactions/Report/c02)
- **The test turns on value.** Commentary says chance and payment are usually present in loot boxes, so the question "largely depends on whether it is played for 'anything else of value'". — [HWL Ebsworth](https://hwlebsworth.com.au/loot-boxes-a-new-form-of-gambling/)
- **Cash-out changes the analysis.** ACMA found DoubleDown was not gambling because there was "no facility to convert or cash out the 'virtual' currency… into 'real' currency". Commentary says that if real-currency exchange were allowed, "it is very likely a different view would be taken". — [Mondaq: Do virtual or online games constitute gambling?](https://webiis08.mondaq.com/australia/gaming/160560/do-virtual-or-online-games-constitute-gambling-the-australian-position)
- **Enforcement against virtual-item gambling.** ACMA issued a formal warning to Feral Holdings over CS:GO Roll, a skins-based casino gambling site. The ACMA chair said gambling sites must follow Australian law even when they use virtual items. — [Gambling.com AU](https://www.gambling.com/au/news/acma-penalizes-skins-online-gambling-site); [Business News Australia](https://direct.businessnewsaustralia.com/articles/acma-takes-aim-at-website-providing-gambling-services-for-counter-strike--skins-.html)
- **Blocking.** ACMA had blocked 1,251 illegal gambling and affiliate websites from November 2019 to June 2025. — [ACMA](https://www.acma.gov.au/articles/2025-06/latest-illegal-online-gambling-websites-blocked)
- **No card-specific action found.** I found no ACMA action naming mystery-box, case-opening or card pack-rip sites. — [iGaming Business, general ACMA blocking coverage](https://igamingbusiness.com/legal-compliance/acma-blocking-gambling-websites-in-australia/)
- **2024 classification changes.**
  - From 22 September 2024, games with paid chance-based in-game purchases get at least an M rating.
  - Games with simulated gambling get at least R18+.
  - The rules apply to games classified from that date.
  - [AGB](https://agbrief.com/news/australia/20/09/2024/australia-tightens-regulations-on-loot-boxes-and-gambling-features-in-video-games/); [PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/australia-sets-new-rules-for-video-games-with-gambling-like-content)
- **ASIC.**
  - ASIC published the revised INFO 225 on 29 October 2025. It moves from "crypto-asset" to "digital assets" terminology. — [Mondo Visione](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/updated-asic-guidance-supports-digital-asset-innovation-and-boosts-investor-prot-20251029/)
  - The guidance explicitly applies to tokenisation of real-world assets and to digital custody. — [Baker McKenzie](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/alerts/2025/11/australia-asic-updates-digital-asset-guidance)
  - Whether a token is a financial product is judged on the token together with its associated "rights, benefits, expectations and product features". If it is a financial product, issuing, dealing or custody requires an Australian financial services licence (AFSL). — [Hall & Wilcox](https://hallandwilcox.com.au/news/asic-issues-long-awaited-guidance-on-digital-assets-and-transitional-relief-for-existing-providers/)
  - The consultation's worked examples included in-game NFTs and membership NFTs. — [Hall & Wilcox (consultation)](https://hallandwilcox.com.au/news/asic-releases-much-anticipated-consultation-paper-to-update-information-sheet-225-on-crypto-assets)
  - Transitional no-action relief was reported to run to 30 June 2026. — [Charltons Quantum](https://charltonsquantum.com/asic-digital-asset-rules-transitional-relief/)
- **US and overseas scrutiny.**
  - ON360, dated 29 August 2026, reports that Whatnot, Collector Crypt and Fanatics face claims that randomised digital card breaks are illegal lotteries. It quotes Collector Crypt's CEO saying up to 98% of opened cards are sold back. The article says it was produced with AI assistance, and I did not verify it against court dockets. — [ON360](https://on360.ca/?p=920)
  - The New York Attorney General sued Valve in February 2026, alleging loot and mystery boxes are illegal gambling. Valve denies it. — [Hoodline](https://hoodline.com/2026/02/ag-james-sues-valve-says-loot-boxes-are-illegal-gambling/); [Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260312-valve-responded-new-york-attorney-mystery-box-lawsuit)
  - Gaming.net calls digital repacks "an emerging online gambling niche". — [Gaming.net](https://www.gaming.net/card-rips-and-repack-market-an-emerging-unusual-online-gambling-niche/)
  - Second Life's gacha ban cites jurisdictions that treat gacha as "unlicensed, unskilled gambling". — [Second Life](https://feedback.secondlife.com/web-features/p/request-for-clarification-on-the-policy-prohibiting-gacha-transactions-2021)

### Inferences
- **Pack rips with buyback likely meet the IGA test.** A digital pack rip has consideration (the pack price) and chance (a random pull). The prize is either cash at a guaranteed 80–90% buyback or a vaulted card with a liquid in-app market. That plausibly satisfies "played for money or anything else of value", which is exactly the element ACMA relied on to exclude loot boxes. A 98% sell-back rate, if accurate, makes the cash-wager character obvious.
- **Two consequences if it is a gambling service:**
  - An online pack-rip for Australians would likely be a prohibited interactive gambling service (a casino-style game of chance), not a licensable product.
  - Offshore incumbents serving Australians, Courtyard included, are exposed to ACMA blocking.
- **Lower-risk designs:**
  - (a) sell specific identified cards at disclosed prices, with buyback, so there is no chance element
  - (b) vault, marketplace and redemption only
  - (c) ship physical sealed packs, which is traditional retail
  - (d) if packs are kept, no operator buyback and no in-app cash-out. This remains arguable because the card itself has value.

  Legal advice is required before any of these.
- **ASIC exposure depends on features.** A 1:1 token that evidences title to a specific vaulted card, with no pooling, yield or lending, looks closer to a warehouse receipt than a financial product. Fractional interests, lending against vaulted cards (Alt-style) or investment-style marketing raise managed-investment-scheme, credit or AFSL questions under INFO 225's "rights and features" test. This is my inference; ASIC was not found to address collectibles specifically.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the exact IGA provisions by search: the s 4 "gambling service" definition, the s 5 prohibited-service categories, and the excluded-lottery and instant-lottery treatment.
- I did not search state gaming and lottery laws (for example, NSW Unlawful Gambling Act 1998 and Vic Gambling Regulation Act 2003) or trade-promotion rules.
- I found no ACMA or state-regulator statement on card pack rips, oripa or mystery-box sites.
- I did not verify the status of Treasury's Digital Asset Platform legislation, or whether ASIC's no-action relief was extended past 30 June 2026.
- The ON360 lawsuit claims (Collector Crypt, Fanatics, Whatnot) are unverified against court records. I found no regulator action naming Courtyard.
